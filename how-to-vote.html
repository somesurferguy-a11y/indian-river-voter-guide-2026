How to vote

The August 18 primary is complete — see the results. Here's everything practical for the November 3 general election in Indian River County.

The Aug 18 primary is over — here's what happened and what's still ahead

Florida is a closed primary state: on August 18, only voters registered with a party could vote in that party's primary contests. Everything below is now history except the November 3 general election, which is what remains for every registered voter regardless of party. See the primary results summary for exactly who won and who's on the Nov 3 ballot.

NPA voters had a real ballot on August 18 too

The Supervisor of Elections states it directly: nonpartisan judicial or school board offices, nonpartisan special districts, and local referendum questions are open to all registered voters, including those without party affiliation.

In Indian River County that meant two genuinely contested races open to every registered voter on August 18:

  • School Board District 2 — Jacqueline Rosario defeated Tom Lange, 51.84%
  • Mosquito Control District Seat 2 — Anna J. Kirkland vs. Craig E. MacCoy; this guide could not confirm a winner as of its last update
  • Plus any judicial races and local referendum questions

Both were decided outright on August 18 — a nonpartisan race with only two candidates does not continue to November.

Note: every other local seat this cycle — all three Hospital District seats, Soil & Water Seat 1, Mosquito Control Seat 3, and School Board Districts 1 and 4 — drew only one candidate and was elected automatically without appearing on the ballot.

Live inside Vero Beach city limits? You have one more race — on Nov 3, not Aug 18.

Correction: this page previously described the Vero Beach City Council race as tied to the August 18 date. That was wrong — it is a nonpartisan "vote for 3" election on November 3 for the three seats currently held by Linda Moore, John Carroll, and Aaron Vos, with eight qualified candidates. It is a municipal race administered by the Vero Beach City Clerk, not the county — it applies only if you live within city limits, not countywide. See the candidate list or confirm at covb.org.

Indian River Shores: confirmed — no municipal election this year

Verified directly on the town's own site: "The Town of Indian River Shores will not be conducting a Municipal Election on November 3, 2026." All three open council seats were filled without opposition — Pat Brier (two-year term), Theresa Morgan (four-year term), and Robert "Rob" Stevenson (four-year term) were seated automatically. There is nothing for Indian River Shores residents to vote on locally this cycle.

Live in Sebastian? Your city election is a week AFTER the Nov 3 general.

Updated Aug 26, 2026: Sebastian runs its own municipal election on a different date than the county/state general — "the second Tuesday following the first Monday in November," which in 2026 falls on November 10, 2026, not November 3. Two of the five council seats are up. This guide could not locate a public list of qualified 2026 candidates — confirm directly with the Sebastian City Clerk (772-388-8215) or cityofsebastian.org.

Live in Fellsmere? You have a contested council race on Nov 3.

Added Aug 26, 2026 — this guide previously missed this race entirely. Fellsmere holds a nonpartisan "vote for 3" election on November 3, 2026 for three City Council seats, with four qualified candidates. See the candidate list or confirm at cityoffellsmere.org.

Orchid: confirmed — no municipal election this year

Updated Aug 26, 2026: exactly three residents filed for the town's three open council seats during the April 2026 qualifying period — Paul Stach, Bill James, and Tracy Cornwell — and all three were unopposed. Per the town's own site, the local council election "will not appear on the ballot in November." There is nothing for Orchid residents to vote on locally this cycle.

The write-in effect — a real rule, often misapplied

Florida has a "universal primary" rule: when all candidates for an office share the same party and the winner will face no opposition in November, the primary opens to every registered voter, NPA included. A single qualified write-in candidate cancels that, because the write-in's blank line counts as November opposition — locking out every voter outside that party.

A common local misconception: this is not what is happening in County Commission District 2. Write-in Nicole Paradiso did qualify there — but a Democrat, Robert Hardingham, also qualified. Because the candidates do not all share one party, that race was never going to be a universal primary, and the write-in changes nothing about who votes in August. The Republican primary there is closed because of Hardingham, not Paradiso.

Ways to vote

1. Vote by mail

You must request a mail ballot — Florida does not send them automatically, and requests no longer carry over across election cycles the way they once did.

  • Request deadline for the primary: August 6 at 5:00 pm.
  • Request deadline for the general election: October 22 at 5:00 pm.
  • Request at voteindianriver.gov or by calling (772) 226-4700.

Returning it: the ballot must be RECEIVED by 7:00 pm on Election Day. A postmark does nothing — a ballot postmarked August 17 that arrives August 19 is not counted. The elections office recommends mailing at least a week ahead.

Mail ballots are NOT accepted at polling places on Election Day. Once early voting ends (Saturday August 15 at 4:00 pm), the only place to hand-deliver one is the Supervisor of Elections office on 43rd Avenue.

Where you can drop it off:

  • Supervisor of Elections office, 4375 43rd Avenue — Mon–Fri 8:30 am–5:00 pm, and 7:00 am–7:00 pm on Election Day.
  • Secure ballot intake stations at all four early voting sites — but only during early voting days and hours. These are staffed; there are no 24-hour unattended drop boxes.

Track your mail ballot → Worth doing — it is faster than waiting to be told there is a problem.

2. Vote early

Any registered voter in the county may vote at any early voting site — you are not tied to your precinct during early voting.

  • Primary: August 8–15.
  • General election: October 19–31.

The four early voting locations:

  • Supervisor of Elections office — 4375 43rd Avenue, Vero Beach, FL 32967
  • Main Library — 1600 21st Street, Vero Beach, FL 32960
  • Intergenerational Recreation Center (iG Center) — 1590 9th Street SW, Vero Beach, FL 32962
  • Sebastian City Hall — 1225 Main Street, Sebastian, FL 32958

Hours — the same at all four sites:

  • 8:00 am – 4:00 pm every day, including both weekends. Weekend hours are not shorter, which is the common assumption.
  • Thursday is the exception: 8:00 am – 6:00 pm. For the primary that is Thursday, August 13.

Note: one page on the elections office website refers to "three" early voting locations while listing four, and two other official pages list four. Four appears correct, but if you are depending on one specific site, call (772) 226-4700 to confirm it is open.

3. Vote on Election Day

Polls are open 7:00 am to 7:00 pm. On Election Day — unlike early voting — you must vote at your assigned precinct.

If you are in line at 7:00 pm, stay in line. Anyone in line when polls close is entitled to vote.

What to bring

Florida requires ID showing both photo and signature. If your photo ID has no signature, bring a second ID that does. The full statutory list (s. 101.043, F.S.) is broader than most voters realize — a debit or credit card counts:

All 12 acceptable forms of ID
  1. Florida driver license
  2. Florida identification card issued by the DHSMV
  3. United States passport
  4. Debit or credit card
  5. Military identification
  6. Student identification
  7. Retirement center identification
  8. Neighborhood association identification
  9. Public assistance identification
  10. Veteran health identification card (U.S. Dept. of Veterans Affairs)
  11. Concealed weapon or firearm license (s. 790.06)
  12. Employee ID issued by any federal, state, county, or municipal entity

No ID? You still vote — and it still counts

You will be given a provisional ballot. Critically: if lack of ID was the only problem, you do not need to bring anything afterward. It is counted as long as you voted in the correct precinct and your signature matches your registration record. Never leave without voting something.

If something goes wrong with your ballot

The cure deadline: 5:00 pm on the second day after the election

That is Thursday, August 20 for the primary and November 5 for the general. It applies if your mail ballot signature does not match your record, or you forgot to sign the envelope.

The fix requires two things by that deadline: a completed Cure Affidavit (Form DS-DE 139) and a copy of your ID. The elections office will notify you — but tracking your own ballot is faster than waiting.

If your signature has changed, or a disability or illness makes it hard to sign consistently, you can file an updated signature with the elections office. Do this before you mail a ballot, not after one is rejected.

Always vote in the precinct matching your legal residence. Voting in the wrong precinct is the most common way a provisional ballot fails.
