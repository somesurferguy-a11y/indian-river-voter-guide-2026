# Relationship mapping

Establishing who is whose what, and being able to defend it: identity resolution and same-name separation,
historical kinship vocabulary, cousin arithmetic, the evidence that actually carries a parent-child link,
charts, numbering, and the integration of DNA with documentary evidence.

**When to load me:** you are deciding whether two records describe one person, arguing a parent-child
link, untangling several men of one name, choosing a chart or numbering system, or deciding what a DNA
match does and does not let you conclude. Date arithmetic and chronology are owned by
`references/timeline-building.md`; citation shapes by `references/citation-management.md`; proof-argument
prose by `references/documentation-standards.md`.

## The exemplar cast

Every example in this file comes from one roster, so any example can be checked against any other. The
people are illustrative; the record types, jurisdictions and repositories around them are structurally
real. Sussex County, Delaware, unless stated.

## 1. The order of operations

```
1. Individuate  -> Is this one person or two? (identity resolution)
2. Separate     -> Which same-named candidate does each record belong to?
3. Assert       -> What relationship does the record claim, in whose words?
4. Evaluate     -> Who informed it, what did they know, what did they want?
5. Correlate    -> Does the whole body of evidence support one relationship, or several?
6. Resolve      -> Account for every conflicting item, in writing.
7. Express      -> Chart it, number it, write it so a reader cannot misread it.
```

Steps 1 and 2 are prerequisites, not preliminaries. **You cannot map a relationship between people you
have not individuated.** A pedigree built on two men merged into one is not a pedigree with an error in
it; it is a pedigree of a person who never existed.

## 2. Identity before relationship

The identity question is: *do these two records describe the same human being?* It is a research question
like any other, it takes evidence, and it takes a written answer. **Identity is argued, not assumed.** The
commonest way a lineage collapses is that someone treated a name match as an identity.

### 2.1 Criteria for arguing identity

Assess each separately, then correlate. No single row settles it.

| Criterion | What you are testing | Strong when | Weak or misleading when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Name form** | Does the name behave the same way across records? | Unusual given name, consistent middle initial, stable spelling in the person's own hand | Name is common locally; clerks spelled phonetically; the name is a patronymic or a saint's name used by half the parish |
| **Age consistency** | Do stated ages and dates describe one life? | Ages in several independent records converge on one birth-year range | Ages are rounded, self-reported, or given by a neighbour; the person had a motive to misstate (militia, pension, marriage, employment) |
| **Place continuity** | Can one person occupy these places in this sequence? | Continuous residence in one jurisdiction with no unexplained gap | Two records place the person in two jurisdictions at overlapping times, which is a collision, not a coincidence |
| **Associates (FAN)** | Do the same neighbours, witnesses, bondsmen and co-litigants recur? | The same witnesses appear across a deed, a will and a marriage bond | The associates are officials who witnessed everything in the county |
| **Occupation / status** | Does the trade, rank or land-holding persist? | A trade uncommon in that place | Generic descriptors (yeoman, labourer, farmer) that fit most adult men |
| **Signature or mark** | Did the person sign, and how? | Consistent signature, or a consistent distinctive mark | Clerks copied documents, so an apparent signature may be the clerk's hand; literacy can change within a life |
| **Distinguishing detail** | Anything unique: a disability, a military unit, a scar noted in a pension file, a specific parcel | The detail is rare and independently recorded | The detail comes from one derivative source everyone copied |

Two tests decide most hard cases. **The land test:** real property is the best identity anchor in
Anglo-American jurisdictions, because a parcel has metes and bounds, adjoining owners and a chain of
title. Follow the parcel, not the name. **The chronology test:** lay every record for the candidate on one
timeline and look for physical impossibility, using the collision test in
`references/timeline-building.md` rather than improvising one here.

### 2.2 Writing the identity argument

Record the decision, not just the outcome.

```
IDENTITY NOTE - Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek
Question:   Is the B. Cray taxed in Sussex Co. 1819-1834 the man whose 1848 will
            was proved there in 1851?
For:        Same parcel (84 a., Bellwether Cr.) in the 1819 deed and the 1848 will
              (proved 1851). [S1][S2]
            Same two witnesses (Teale, Marchbank) to the 1819 deed and the 1848
              will (proved 1851). [S1][S2]
            Age in 1850 census (62) consistent with age at the 1810 marriage. [S3][S4]
Against:    1827 tax list shows two B. Crays in the same hundred. [S5]
Resolved:   The second is Bartholomew Cray of Slaughter Neck, taxed on a different
              parcel, d. 1839 (adm. bond). [S5][S6] Assignment of the 1827 entries
              follows the parcel. See SEPARATION FILE: Cray, Bartholomew.
Conclusion: One man. Confidence strong. Revisit if a third parcel appears.
Sources:    S1 [deed: full reference note]   S2 [will: full reference note]
            S3 [1850 census: full note]      S4 [1810 marriage: full note]
            S5 [1827 tax list: full note]    S6 [adm. bond: full note]
3 May 2026 / [researcher]
```

Every assertion carries its own citation slot, because the note is the thing a successor re-checks;
shapes for what goes in the slots are owned by `references/citation-management.md`.

Two rules keep this honest. **Never merge on a name plus one shared detail**; merging is destructive and
hard to undo once it propagates into charts and online trees. And **record a split as carefully as a
merge**: "these are two men" needs the same evidence and the same write-up as "these are one man."

## 3. Same-name separation

Communities routinely held three, five, nine men of one name: cousins named for a grandfather, unrelated
migrants from the same parish, a Welsh or Scandinavian patronymic recycling two forenames every
generation, Spanish and Portuguese conventions reusing both surnames. The discipline is the same
everywhere.

1. **Define the study universe** first: one jurisdiction, one date span, one name in all its
spellings. Write both boundaries down. Widening later is fine; pretending you never narrowed is not.
2. **Reconstruct every candidate, not the one you want.** Build a skeleton for each man of the
name, however irrelevant he looks. The candidate you skip owns your record.
3. **Collect every record in the universe**, including ones that plainly belong to someone else.
You cannot assign records you have not gathered.
4. **Assign each record to a candidate and defend the assignment** in one line, naming the
criterion from 2.1 that did the work: parcel, associates, age, trade.
5. **Keep an explicit unassigned pile.** Records you cannot place stay in a labelled list. They
are not noise, and they are not evidence for your preferred candidate. An unassigned pile that never
shrinks is telling you a candidate is missing.
6. **Run the collision test** across candidates: one man recorded in two places at one time
proves a separation, not a contradiction. The test lives in `references/timeline-building.md`.
7. **Publish the separation.** It is a research product in its own right and usually deserves a
proof argument (`references/documentation-standards.md`).

```
NAME UNDER STUDY: Bartholomew Cray (also Crey, Craye, Kray)
UNIVERSE: Sussex County, Delaware, 1795-1860

A  "of Bellwether Creek"  b. ca. 1788, d. 1851, cooper, m. Susannah Teale
B  "of Slaughter Neck"    b. ca. 1790, d. 1839, farmer, m. (2) Dorcas (Wake) Kell
C  "the younger"          b. ca. 1812, son of A, removed to Ohio ca. 1840

RECORD                           ASSIGNED    BASIS                          CITATION
1819 deed, 84 a. Bellwether Cr.  A           parcel; witnesses Teale,       [S1]
                                               Marchbank
1827 tax, Cedar Creek Hd.        B           hundred; no land matches A's   [S5]
                                               parcel
1830 census, Broadkill Hd.       A           household composition fits A   [S7]
1838 marr. bond, surety B. Cray  UNASSIGNED  A and C both plausible; hand   [S8]
                                               unclear
1841 Ohio deed, B. Cray          C           A taxed in Sussex in 1841      [S9][S10]
```

**A separation file with no citation column is a claim, not a record.** The bracketed keys resolve in
the file's source list; a reader who cannot re-fetch the record cannot test the assignment.

**Anti-patterns.** Assuming the eldest candidate is yours. Assigning the flattering records (the
officeholder, the large estate) to your ancestor and the rest to a shadow candidate. Letting a record
migrate from unassigned to assigned because the argument needed it.

## 4. Kinship terminology and its historical drift

A relationship word in a record is a word in its writer's vocabulary, not yours. Below: the modern
reading, the historical reading to test for, and the era and place where that reading applies. **These
usages are era-and-place specific; do not port a reading from an English will of 1660 to an Ohio will of
1880.** Where a row says "chiefly," treat the older sense as a possibility to test, not a default.

| Term | Modern reading | Historical reading to test for | Era / place where it applies |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cousin** | child of an aunt or uncle | almost any collateral kin: nephew, niece, grandchild, kin by marriage; also a courtesy address between social equals and in royal correspondence | England and colonial North America, chiefly 16th-18th c.; survives later in rural and formal usage |
| **cousin-german** | (rare today) | first cousin, from Latin *germanus*, "of the same parents" | English and Scots legal usage, medieval to 19th c. |
| **brother-german / sister-german** | (rare today) | full sibling, sharing both parents, as against a half sibling | English and Scots legal usage |
| **nephew / niece** | child of a sibling | descendant generally, and specifically **grandson / granddaughter**, from Latin *nepos* | English, chiefly through c. 1600, attested into the 17th c.; check any pre-1700 English will |
| **junior / senior (Jr, Sr)** | son and father | the younger and the older of two living men of one name **in the same community**: often kin, frequently uncle and nephew or cousins, sometimes unrelated. The labels are relative and they **move**: when the elder dies the younger becomes Senior and a third man becomes Junior | England and North America, 17th-19th c.; the father-son convention hardens only in the later 19th c. |
| **"the third", "tertius"** | third in a line | the third living man of the name in the community, on the same relative logic | New England town and church records especially |
| **"now wife"** | current wife, implying a former one | **It is not evidence of a former wife.** It is a scrivener's phrase fixing the bequest on the wife living at the date of the will so that it cannot pass to a **later** wife should the testator remarry after executing it, and defining her life estate or dower precisely. Many scriveners wrote it formulaically. Where a prior marriage did exist it had most often ended at the first wife's death, but do not treat that as universal: divorce was available in some jurisdictions within this band, including colonial Massachusetts Bay and Connecticut, England by private act of Parliament and later by judicial divorce under the Matrimonial Causes Act, and many US states by statute during the nineteenth century | English and American wills, 17th-19th c. |
| **father-in-law / mother-in-law** | parent of one's spouse | **stepfather / stepmother** | English and American, 17th to early 19th c. |
| **son-in-law / daughter-in-law** | spouse of one's child | **stepson / stepdaughter** (sometimes alongside the modern sense in one document) | same |
| **brother / sister** | sibling | sibling-in-law; fellow member of a church, meeting, lodge or company | widespread; in Quaker, Moravian and Baptist records the religious sense is the default |
| **natural child** | biological child | two attested senses: (a) a child born outside marriage; (b) a child of the body as against a stepchild or adopted child. Context and jurisdiction decide | (a) common in English and American legal and parish usage 17th c. onward; (b) also attested throughout |
| **base born, "spurious", "filius populi"** | (archaic) | born outside marriage; the phrasing is the register keeper's or the court's and carries consequences for settlement and inheritance | English parish registers and quarter sessions, 16th-19th c.; American colonial and state courts |
| **of the half blood** | (legal, current) | sharing one parent only. A statutory term: intestacy statutes in England and in the American states treated half and whole blood differently, and the difference varied by jurisdiction and date | English common law and derived US state statutes |
| **relict** | (archaic) | widow, occasionally widower | English and American probate, 17th-19th c. |
| **orphan** | child with no living parent | in many American jurisdictions and in English parish apprenticeship, a **fatherless** child whose mother may be living and named in the same file | US orphans' courts and county guardianship; English poor-law apprenticeship |
| **kinsman / kinswoman** | (archaic) | a relative whose exact connection the writer chose not to state or did not know; often a connection through a female line | English and American wills |
| **"removed"** | generational offset between cousins | the same, but often mis-stated: removal counts **generations**, never lateral distance | universal |
| **double first cousins** | (current) | children of two siblings of one family who married two siblings of another. They share all four grandparents, not two | universal |
| **Mrs. / Mistress; Goodman / Goodwife** | marital title | rank markers. "Mrs." denoted social standing and did not reliably indicate marriage | England and colonial New England, 17th-18th c. |
| **friend** | (non-kin) | in Quaker records, a member of the Society of Friends; in wills often the executor or a trustee, occasionally a relative not named as kin | Quaker records 17th c. onward |
| **uncle / aunt** | sibling of a parent | courtesy titles for any older relative or respected elder, and in the 19th-c. American South also imposed by white speakers on Black adults as a racialised form of address | note era and community explicitly when using such a record |

**How to use this.** Log the record's word *verbatim*, then log your reading of it separately, with the
reason. Never silently translate.

```
Record:      Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek, will executed 1848 (proved 1851)
Source says: "to my cousin Silas Cray"
Read as:     an unspecified collateral kinsman. Silas Cray (1825-1901) is the son of
             Bartholomew Cray of Slaughter Neck, whose kinship to the testator is not
             established. Delaware in 1848 is late for the widest sense of "cousin"
             but not outside it, so the row's era note applies: test it, do not assume it
Confidence:  low; first cousin once removed, nephew and kin-by-marriage all remain open
Effect:      the word is a lead into the separation file, not a link
```

## 5. Cousin arithmetic

### 5.1 The computation

1. Find the **nearest common ancestor** (NCA). If two people have more than one independent NCA
couple, see 5.4.
2. Count generations **up** from each to the NCA. Call the smaller `a` and the larger `b`.
3. Apply:

| Case | Relationship |
|---|---|
| `a = 0` | direct line: one is the ancestor of the other, `b` generations up |
| `a = 1` | the shorter-path person is the sibling of an ancestor of the other: **sibling** if `b = 1`, **aunt/uncle** if `b = 2`, **granduncle/grandaunt** if `b = 3`, **great-granduncle/grandaunt** if `b = 4`, adding one "great" per further generation |
| `a >= 2` | **cousin degree = `a - 1`**, **removal = `b - a`** |

Removal measures difference in generation and nothing else. In the grid below, rows and columns are
generations down from the common ancestor, which is the same count as the arrows in a path chain.

|  | child (1) | grandchild (2) | gt-grandchild (3) | 2x-gt-grandchild (4) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **child (1)** | siblings | aunt/uncle - niece/nephew | grandaunt/uncle - grandniece/nephew | gt-grandaunt/uncle - gt-grandniece/nephew |
| **grandchild (2)** | | 1st cousins | 1st cousins once removed | 1st cousins twice removed |
| **gt-grandchild (3)** | | | 2nd cousins | 2nd cousins once removed |
| **2x-gt-grandchild (4)** | | | | 3rd cousins |

The grid stops at four generations for width, not for arithmetic. Each further column adds one removal and
nothing else, so a great-grandchild (3) against a 3x-great-grandchild (5) is second cousins twice removed,
and a child (1) against a 3x-great-grandchild (5) is a 2x-great-grandaunt or granduncle. Both worked
examples below sit in that fifth column, and both can be read off the grid by extending it one step.

### 5.2 Worked example A

Common ancestor **Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek (1788-1851)**. **Tabitha Stannard (1871-1944)** is
his great-granddaughter, three generations down (Tabitha -> her father Reuben Stannard -> her grandmother
Mary (Cray) Stannard -> Bartholomew), so `a = 3`. **Josiah Teale (1919-1986)** is his 3x-great-grandson,
five generations down (Josiah -> his father -> his grandfather -> his great-grandmother Ann (Cray) Teale ->
his 2x-great-grandfather Bartholomew the younger -> Bartholomew), so `b = 5`. Degree `= a - 1 = 2`;
removal `= b - a = 2`. **Second cousins twice removed**, which is what the grid gives at row 3, column 5.

**Count arrows, not names: each arrow is one generation.** A chain showing four arrows cannot support
`b = 5`, and a chain miscounted by one is the commonest way this calculation fails. Count the chain, then
check the label against the grid; if they disagree, the chain is usually right and the label wrong.

Written out: "Josiah Teale (1919-1986) and Tabitha Stannard (1871-1944) were second cousins twice removed,
both descending from Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek (1788-1851): Tabitha through his daughter Mary,
Josiah through his son Bartholomew the younger." Note what the bare label hides and the sentence supplies,
namely *which child of the NCA each descends through*. Always supply it.

### 5.3 Worked example B (the `a = 1` case that breaks the cousin formula)

Same family, one step sideways. Common ancestor **Susannah (Teale) Cray (1791-1868)**, wife of the
Bartholomew in 5.2. **Mary (Cray) Stannard (1810-1884)** is her daughter, so `a = 1`. **Josiah Teale
(1919-1986)** is her 3x-great-grandson by the same five-arrow chain used in 5.2, so `b = 5`. Because
`a = 1`, do not say "zeroth cousins four times removed." Mary is the sibling of Josiah's ancestor
`b - 1 = 4` generations up, that is, of his 2x-great-grandparent Bartholomew the younger. **Mary is
Josiah's 2x-great-grandaunt; Josiah is her 2x-great-grandnephew**, which is row 1, column 5 of the grid.
Prefer *grandaunt* and *granduncle* to *great-aunt* and *great-uncle*, which are also used loosely for any
aunt or uncle of an older generation, and say which convention your document uses.

The two examples share a cast deliberately: Tabitha in 5.2 is Mary's granddaughter, so if 5.2 and 5.3
disagreed about where Mary sits, one of them would be wrong and you could see it. Build your own examples
so they check against each other.

**Direction is not optional.** "First cousin once removed" describes both *your parent's first cousin* and
*your first cousin's child*. Write the direction, or name the path.

### 5.4 Double cousins and multiple relationships

If two brothers marry two sisters, their children are **double first cousins**: they share all four
grandparents and descend through two independent NCA couples. Double first cousins share, on average,
about as much autosomal DNA as half siblings, which is why an unexplained "half sibling range" result in
an intermarried community is often a double cousin. Test also for a widower marrying his deceased wife's
sister, and for successive generations of two families intermarrying.

**Rule:** when two people are related by more than one path, state **every** path. Write "they were double
first cousins (their fathers were brothers; their mothers were sisters)," never just "cousins."

## 6. Half, step, adoptive and fostered relationships

These are the relationships readers most often misread, because the shorthand drops the qualifier. The fix
is a writing convention applied without exception.

| Relationship | Definition | Write it as | Never write |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Half sibling** | one shared parent | "his half brother Silas Cray (same father, Bartholomew Cray of Slaughter Neck)" | "his brother" |
| **Step relation** | connection by a parent's marriage, no shared ancestry | "his stepdaughter Patience Kell, daughter of his wife Dorcas by her first husband Jabez Kell" | "his daughter" |
| **Step in an older record** | the record may say "son-in-law" or "father-in-law" meaning step | quote the word, then give your reading: "described as his 'son-in-law', read here as stepson" | a silent modernisation |
| **Adoptive (legal)** | adoption effected by court or statute | "adopted son, per the adoption decree entered in the [county] court, 1889" | "son" unqualified |
| **Adoptive (informal)** | "taken to raise," no legal act | "raised in the household of, and called son by, Bartholomew Cray of Slaughter Neck; no adoption record found in [jurisdiction], [date span]" | "adopted son" |
| **Fostered / bound out / apprenticed** | a labour or maintenance arrangement | "bound to Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek by the overseers of the poor, 1827 (indenture)" | any kinship term |
| **Guardianship** | a legal office, often held by kin, often not | "guardian (an office, not necessarily a blood relationship)" | "uncle," inferred from guardianship alone |
| **Genetic vs. documentary parent** | when DNA and documents disagree | name both: "documentary father" and "genetic father," each defended | choosing one and deleting the other |

In charts that cannot express a qualifier, carry it as a bracketed tag on the name: `Patience Kell
[step]`, `Silas Cray [half, same father]`, `Nehemiah Cray [adopted 1889]`. Put the legend on the chart
itself, not in a separate file.

## 7. What proves a parent-child link

### 7.1 A stated relationship is an assertion by an informant

When a record says "my son Josiah" or "father: Bartholomew Cray," it is not reporting a biological certainty.
It is transmitting a claim made by a particular person, at a particular moment, for a particular purpose.
Evaluate three things every time.

- **Who informed it?** Name them if the record names them. "Informant not stated" is a finding,
not a blank.
- **What could they know?** Present at the event, or repeating what they were told decades later?
The distinction is *primary / secondary / undetermined information*, assessed **per assertion**. A death
certificate is routinely primary for the date of death and secondary for the decedent's parents' names,
because the informant witnessed one and not the other.
- **What did they want?** A pension claimant wants a pension; a will contestant wants a share; a
remarrying widow wants her dower. Motive does not make an assertion false; it tells you what to
corroborate.

Direct evidence of parentage answers the parentage question on its own. Its characteristic failure modes:
the informant did not know; the informant confused a same-named person; the clerk transcribed wrongly; the
relationship word means something other than you assume (section 4); the "original" is in fact a
derivative copy.

### 7.2 Indirect evidence assembled into a relationship argument

**Direct and indirect are not properties of a record.** The same item is direct evidence for one question
and indirect for another, because evidence is a relationship between an information item and the question
you are asking. A marriage bond naming a bride's father is direct evidence of that father-daughter link
and indirect evidence of the bride's age, since consent implies minority. Type the evidence against the
question, never against the document.

Where no record states the relationship, the link is built from items that individually prove something
else: land transferred for nominal consideration; a bondsman or surety who is a likely kinsman; naming
patterns within a documented local custom; household composition across successive censuses; recurring
witnesses; a distinctive parcel descending intact.

Indirect arguments live or die on **eliminating alternatives**. "This must be his son" is not an argument
until you have enumerated the other men who could have been the grantee and shown why each fails. The
written form belongs to `references/documentation-standards.md`; the raw material is here.

### 7.3 Record types that carry relationship assertions

| Record type | What it typically asserts | Characteristic reliability | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Will / probate (testate)** | children, spouse, grandchildren, sometimes sons-in-law | High: the testator knew his own family and had a legal motive for precision | Omits children already provided for, estranged or predeceased; names daughters under married surnames; groups grandchildren as "the children of my deceased son"; may reach you as an abstract, not the full text |
| **Administration / intestate distribution** | heirs at law, often with relationship and residence | Very high for *heirship*, because the distribution is statutory | Heirship is a legal category, not a biological one: half blood, stepchildren and children born outside marriage are treated differently by statute and jurisdiction |
| **Deed of gift, "for love and affection"** | a close relationship, usually named | High: the nominal consideration is the point, and the grantor is the informant | The relationship word may be loose ("cousin"); a gift can go to a non-relative; recording may postdate execution by years |
| **Guardianship / orphans' court** | the minor's father is dead; the minor's age bracket; often the mother and the whole sibling group | High for fatherlessness and age; often the best sibling list available | "Orphan" may mean fatherless only; a guardian is an office and need not be kin; in many jurisdictions a child above a statutory age chose his own guardian, so the choice shows preference, not descent. Verify the statute for that place and date |
| **Church baptism with sponsors** | in Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Dutch and many German traditions the sponsors are frequently close kin, making the list a kinship map | Good as a *pointer*, strong where the community's sponsorship custom is documented | The custom is community- and era-specific; sponsors may be chosen for status or patronage. Establish the local custom before leaning on it |
| **Marriage bond / licence / banns** | parental or guardian consent for a minor; a surety often related | Good; recorded consent is a direct relationship assertion | The surety may be a professional bondsman or the bride's employer; "consent of father" may be clerk's boilerplate |
| **Civil birth / marriage / death registration** | parents' names | Varies sharply by assertion and informant | Parents' names on a death registration are secondary information from an informant who may be a grandchild, an in-law, a neighbour or a clerk. Identify the informant and their stated relationship before weighting it |
| **Obituary / funeral notice** | survivors, often with relationships and residences | Useful for the survivor list and for locating living kin at a date | Informant usually unnamed; written under time pressure; step- and half- relations reported as full; second wives conflated with mothers |
| **Pension application** (US Revolutionary War, 1812, Civil War; UK service and Chelsea or Greenwich pension records) | marriage, widowhood, children's births, often transcribed Bible pages and sworn affidavits from kin and neighbours | Among the richest relationship files existing for ordinary people | The claimant has a direct financial motive; affidavits often postdate events by decades; ages and dates drift toward whatever qualifies the claim |
| **Census household** | co-residence, and from the later 19th c. an explicit relationship-to-head column (US from 1880; England and Wales from 1851) | Good for household structure | "Son" in that column can be stepson or son-in-law; boarders and servants may be kin; the informant may have been a neighbour |
| **Bastardy bond / filiation order / poor-law examination** | a named putative father | Direct and often reliable, because a parish or county wanted the money | Naming was contested; a named man is an accusation until corroborated |
| **Family Bible** | births, marriages, deaths | Can be excellent | Date the *Bible* (its imprint date) against the entries: entries predating the imprint were copied later, from memory or another source. That is a chronology test, see `references/timeline-building.md` |

One citation, so the table has something to point at. Shapes and the layered/provenance model are owned by
`references/citation-management.md`; bracketed items are placeholders you fill from the record in hand.

```
First (full) reference note:
  Sussex County, Delaware, Deed Book K: 218-219, Bartholomew Cray to Mary
  (Cray) Stannard, deed of gift, 14 March 1831; Recorder of Deeds, Georgetown,
  Delaware; digital images, "[collection title exactly as the site gives it],"
  [website name] ([site URL] : accessed 3 May 2026), image [n] of [N], citing
  [the film or original the site names].

Shortened note:
  Sussex Co., Del., Deed Bk K: 218-219, Cray to Stannard, 1831.

Source-list entry:
  Sussex County, Delaware. Deed Books. Recorder of Deeds, Georgetown, Delaware.
  Digital images. [Website name]. [Site URL].
```

The probate and guardianship rows are US-specific in their court names. The non-US analogues: in England
and Wales, wills proved before 1858 in the ecclesiastical courts (the Prerogative Court of Canterbury and
the diocesan and archdeaconry courts) and afterwards in the civil probate registries; in Scotland,
testaments and inventories recorded by the commissary and later sheriff courts, with tutory and curatory
standing where an American researcher looks for guardianship.

### 7.4 Negative evidence in relationship work

**Negative evidence** is the absence of something where the records would lead you to expect its presence.
It is not a **negative search result**, which is a search that found nothing; say which you mean.
(Negative search results are handled in `references/research-workflows.md`.)

The classic case: *a child is absent from a parent's will.* Before that absence supports "not his child"
or "estranged," eliminate every innocent explanation.

1. The child **predeceased** the testator: check burials, the child's own probate, and any bequest
to "the children of my deceased son."
2. The child was **already provided for** by a deed of gift, an advancement or a marriage portion.
Search land and probate indexes under the child's name *before* the will's date.
3. The child is there **under another name**: a daughter by her married surname, a son by a middle
name or nickname, a person named only as "my grandchild."
4. You are reading an **abstract, index entry or published summary**, not the full instrument.
5. The will is **old**: executed years before death, before a later child was born or a marriage
took place. Compare the execution date, not the probate date.
6. The will disposes of **only part of the estate**; land may have passed by earlier deed, entail,
survivorship or intestacy.
7. The child was a **minor**, or under a legal disability, handled by a separate arrangement.
8. There is **another testator of the same name** (section 3).

Only when those are eliminated in writing does the absence become evidence, and it is then stated as such:
"The absence of Levin Cray (1814-1889) from the 1848 will of Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek (proved
1851) is negative evidence bearing on the claimed relationship; explanations 1 through 8 were tested and
eliminated as follows..." In the roster at the head of this file the answer is explanation 8 in its
commonest form: Levin is the son of Bartholomew Cray of Slaughter Neck, and the tree that made him a
Bellwether Creek son had merged two men of one name.

## 8. Charts, and when each earns its place

Every chart is a compression. The danger is never what it shows; it is what it silently drops, because
researchers reason from the picture in front of them.

| Chart | The question it answers | What it hides, and the trouble that causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pedigree / ancestor** | Who are one person's direct ancestors? | Siblings, multiple marriages, childless lines, the whole FAN network, and **the evidence**. Every line looks equally solid, so an unproven link is indistinguishable from a proven one. Annotate confidence on the chart or do not trust it |
| **Descendant** | Who descends from one couple? | Ancestors, in-laws' families, and remarried descendants' other spouses. Grows explosively and encourages merging of same-named descendants |
| **Hourglass** | Ancestors and descendants of one subject together | Collaterals on the ancestral half, which are exactly the people whose records usually solve the problem |
| **Fan** | How complete is my direct ancestry, at a glance? | Everything but direct ancestry, and it compresses names to fit, losing distinguishing detail. Excellent for spotting gaps, useless for reasoning |
| **Drop-line** | What is the single line from A to B? (lineage-society applications) | Everything off the line, including the siblings whose records prove the line. A drop-line is an output, never a research tool |
| **FAN / cluster network** | Who associated with whom, and how often? | **Chronology.** It flattens twenty years into one picture, so it can connect two people who never overlapped, and it tempts the reader to read association as kinship. Date every edge and check the diagram against the timeline (`references/timeline-building.md`) |
| **Family group sheet** | What is known about one nuclear family, with sources? | Wider context. But it is the one form that forces a source per assertion, which is why it remains the working unit |

**Do your reasoning on the group sheet and the timeline; use charts to communicate a conclusion you have
already reached.** A chart drawn to figure something out will tell you what you drew.

## 9. Numbering systems

Numbering exists so a reader can find a person and two documents can refer to one person unambiguously.
Any system does that if you **declare it**. Failing to declare it is the real error.

### 9.1 Ahnentafel (ascending, for ancestors)

The subject is 1. For any person numbered `n`:

```
father of n = 2n        mother of n = 2n + 1        child of n = n div 2
```

- Every male ancestor has an **even** number and every female an **odd** one; only number 1 can be
either.
- Generation of `n` is `floor(log2 n) + 1`; generation `g` runs from `2^(g-1)` to `2^g - 1` and
holds `2^(g-1)` slots. So 2 and 3 are the parents, 4 through 7 the grandparents, 8 through 15 the
great-grandparents, 16 through 31 the 2x-great-grandparents.
- The number alone gives the path. 19 is odd, so female; her child is 19 div 2 = 9, also female;
9's child is 4, male; 4's child is 2, male; 2's child is 1. Number 19 is the subject's father's father's
mother's mother.
- **Pedigree collapse is visible as arithmetic:** one person occupying two Ahnentafel numbers means
two lines have converged. Flag it, because it changes DNA expectations (10.5).
- Ahnentafel cannot express siblings, multiple marriages, uncertainty or half relationships. It is
an index, not an argument.

### 9.2 Descending systems: one family, numbered four ways

> **Bartholomew Cray** (1788-1851), m. Susannah Teale. Children: Mary (1810-1884), Bartholomew the
> younger (1812-1880, m. Verity Marchbank, with children Josiah and Ann), Hosea (1815-1816).

**Register system** (the New England Historical and Genealogical Register style). Arabic numbers go
**only** to persons whose descendants are carried forward to a later entry; all children get lower-case
roman numerals in birth order, and the carried-forward child's Arabic number sits to the left of its roman
numeral. A superscript figure after a given name is the generation.

```
1. BARTHOLOMEW(1) CRAY, b. 1788, d. 1851; m. SUSANNAH TEALE.
   Children of Bartholomew(1) and Susannah (Teale) Cray:
        i.   MARY(2) CRAY, b. 1810, d. 1884.
   2.   ii.  BARTHOLOMEW(2) CRAY, b. 1812, d. 1880.
        iii. HOSEA(2) CRAY, b. 1815, d. 1816.

2. BARTHOLOMEW(2) CRAY (Bartholomew(1)), b. 1812, d. 1880; m. VERITY MARCHBANK.
   Children of Bartholomew(2) and Verity (Marchbank) Cray:
        i.   JOSIAH(3) CRAY, b. 1840.
        ii.  ANN(3) CRAY, b. 1843.
```

Mary and Hosea never receive an Arabic number, so there is no number by which to cite Mary.

**NGSQ / Record system** (National Genealogical Society Quarterly style). **Every** child receives an
Arabic number in one running sequence, and a plus sign marks those carried forward.

```
1. BARTHOLOMEW(1) CRAY, b. 1788, d. 1851; m. SUSANNAH TEALE.
   Children of Bartholomew(1) and Susannah (Teale) Cray:
       2.  i.   MARY(2) CRAY, b. 1810, d. 1884.
   +   3.  ii.  BARTHOLOMEW(2) CRAY, b. 1812, d. 1880.
       4.  iii. HOSEA(2) CRAY, b. 1815, d. 1816.

3. BARTHOLOMEW(2) CRAY (Bartholomew(1)), b. 1812, d. 1880; m. VERITY MARCHBANK.
   Children of Bartholomew(2) and Verity (Marchbank) Cray:
       5.  i.   JOSIAH(3) CRAY, b. 1840.
       6.  ii.  ANN(3) CRAY, b. 1843.
```

Mary is now "no. 2" and can be cited, indexed and cross-referenced although her line stops. That is the
whole practical difference, and it is why NGSQ suits a complete descendancy while Register suits a
narrative following selected lines.

**A caution on the name "Modified Register."** The literature uses it inconsistently: some authors mean
the NGSQ/Record system, others mean Register with plus signs added. Because the label is ambiguous,
**declare the mechanics, not just the name** (9.3).

**Henry** (progenitor 1; each child appends its birth-order digit) and **d'Aboville** (the same, with
periods between generations, so no special convention is needed past nine children):

```
Henry            d'Aboville
1     Bartholomew Cray            1
11    Mary                        1.1
12    Bartholomew the younger     1.2
121     Josiah                    1.2.1
122     Ann                       1.2.2
13    Hosea                       1.3
```

Because Henry concatenates digits, a family of more than nine children needs a convention: the classic
Henry system uses X for a tenth child, then A, B, C for the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth; "modified
Henry" instead parenthesises the multi-digit number, as `1(10)`. Henry and d'Aboville numbers are
self-describing, since the number *is* the path from the progenitor. Register and NGSQ numbers are
arbitrary sequence numbers carrying no path, which is why those systems put the ancestral line in
parentheses after the name.

### 9.3 Declaring your system

Put a mechanical declaration near the front of any compiled genealogy.

```
NUMBERING. Descendants are numbered in the NGSQ (Record) style: every child
receives an Arabic number in a single running sequence, and a plus sign before
the number marks a child whose family is carried forward to its own numbered
entry. Roman numerals give birth order within each family. A superscript figure
after a given name is the generation, counting the progenitor as generation one.
Ancestors, where cited, use Ahnentafel numbers relative to [subject], who is 1.
```

If your software emits a numbering you did not choose, name the software and the setting; that is an
honest declaration and lets a reader reproduce it.

## 10. DNA and documentary evidence together

DNA testing changes what is provable and changes nothing about how proof works. A genetic result is
information from a source, evaluated and correlated like any other, and a conclusion resting on it must
still satisfy the Genealogical Proof Standard. What DNA adds is a second, independent line of evidence;
what it does not add is certainty about a *specific* relationship.

### 10.1 The governing rule

> **A DNA match supports a relationship hypothesis only in combination with a documentary
> framework.** Shared DNA establishes that two testers share ancestry. It does not say through
> whom, in which generation, or by which path. The documentary work supplies the candidate paths;
> the DNA discriminates among them, and sometimes excludes them all.

The corollary: **DNA excludes more decisively than it confirms.** A Y-DNA mismatch between two men
documented as patrilineal cousins is a hard result. A shared autosomal amount "consistent with second
cousins" is a soft one, because it is consistent with a dozen other relationships too.

### 10.2 Autosomal DNA: ranges, not relationships

A child inherits about half of each parent's autosomal DNA, but *which* half is decided by recombination,
which is random. Three consequences drive everything.

- Beyond the parent-child pair, the amount two relatives share is a **random draw from a
distribution**, not a fixed figure. Two pairs in the same documented relationship can share markedly
different amounts.
- The distributions for different relationships **overlap heavily**, and the overlap widens with
distance. This is why the standard tools report a *range of possible relationships with associated
probabilities* rather than a relationship.
- Far enough out, true relatives share **no detectable DNA at all**. Absence of a match between
distant cousins is expected, not disproof. Absence where a match was near-certain is a finding that needs
explaining (section 11).

**Therefore:** never write "they share X cM, so they are second cousins." Write "the shared amount falls
in a range whose most probable relationships include second cousins, first cousins once removed, and half
first cousins; the documentary evidence eliminates the second and third because [reason]." Report the
measured figure and the tool that produced the range, and treat published thresholds as conventions rather
than boundaries. Tools and authors set different minimum segment-size floors for exactly this reason:
**state the floor you used** instead of assuming your reader shares it.

### 10.3 Clustering and triangulation

**Shared-match (in-common-with) clustering** groups matches by which of them match each other. Clusters
usually correspond to ancestral lines, which makes clustering the fastest way to turn a list of names into
a set of hypotheses. A cluster tells you *that* a group shares ancestry; it does not name the ancestor.

**Triangulation** is the stronger claim, and it needs all four of: three or more testers sharing **the
same segment at the same chromosome position**; those testers matching **each other** on that segment;
documented pedigrees that **converge on one ancestral couple**; and segment data, meaning a platform with
a chromosome browser or comparison tool. Two people matching a third at the same position without matching
each other there is not a triangulated group.

Hazards: segments below your chosen floor are frequently **identical by state** (coincidental) rather than
**identical by descent**; some chromosome regions are known pile-up regions that generate spurious
matches; reported matches are half-identical regions, so a match may be on either parent's side unless the
data are phased. **Testing the oldest generation available is the highest-value action in genetic
genealogy**, because a parent's or aunt's results phase everything below them.

### 10.4 Y-DNA and mitochondrial DNA

| | Y-DNA | Mitochondrial DNA |
|---|---|---|
| **Who carries it** | biological males | everyone |
| **Who passes it on** | father to son | mother to all her children |
| **Line tracked** | the strict paternal line | the strict maternal line |
| **Genealogical use** | surname studies and patrilineal-line questions where surnames descend patrilineally; confirming or excluding a documented male line | confirming or excluding a documented female line; identifying remains; linking families where the female line is otherwise undocumented |
| **Resolution** | STR panels give a genetic distance between testers and a probabilistic estimate of generations to a common ancestor; SNP testing places a man on a branch of the Y haplotree, giving finer and better-dated placement | a very slow mutation rate, so a full-sequence match may mean a common maternal ancestor within a genealogical timeframe or many centuries earlier. Low resolution for pinpointing a generation |
| **Blind spot** | says nothing about any line but the strict paternal one, a vanishing fraction of a pedigree | the same, for the strict maternal one |

Both are strong as **exclusions** and as **line tests**, weak as generation-finders. The X chromosome has
a restricted inheritance path as well (a man inherits his X only from his mother), so X matching can
exclude whole branches as the source of a match: useful as a filter, never a proof on its own.

### 10.5 Endogamy and pedigree collapse

In a population that married within itself for generations, two people are related along many paths at
once, so the DNA they share is the sum of many small contributions. Shared amounts are **inflated relative
to the single documented relationship**, and a tool's relationship range runs systematically too close.
Pedigree collapse (cousins marrying, so one ancestor occupies more than one Ahnentafel slot) does the same
inside a single family.

Handling: say in writing that the population is endogamous or that the pedigree collapses; do not use
shared-amount ranges as though calibrated for that population; lean harder on segment triangulation and on
documentary reconstruction of the whole community; and expect large, tangled clusters rather than clean
per-line ones.

### 10.6 Standards, citation and reporting

The Board for Certification of Genealogists brought DNA evidence inside *Genealogy Standards* in the
second edition, adding standards for DNA work and modifying existing ones, and the revised second edition
further clarified the DNA and privacy standards; the Genealogist's Code was updated to address protection
of people who provide DNA samples. Described by content rather than by number, what they require is what
this section has argued: that DNA evidence be **planned for** like any other evidence, **analysed and
correlated with the documentary evidence** rather than presented beside it, that conflicts between DNA and
documents be **resolved**, and that DNA results be **cited** so another researcher could return to them.

A DNA citation follows the same layered logic as any other source: who tested, what test, on which
platform, what the result was, when it was viewed, and where it can be found. Living testers are
ordinarily identified by initials or by a described relationship, not by name. Shapes belong to
`references/citation-management.md`. A DNA-bearing conclusion states in the text: the test type; who was
tested and their documented relationship to the subject; the measured result and the tool that interpreted
it; the relationship hypotheses the result is consistent with; and the documentary evidence that
eliminated the alternatives.

## 11. Misattributed parentage

**How it presents.** A tester matches the expected relatives on one parental side and none on the other.
Y-DNA does not match other men of the documented surname line, or matches a different surname cluster
consistently. Shared matches cluster around a surname absent from the pedigree, often a family living in
the same place at the same time. Expected close matches are entirely missing where the documented
relationship was close enough to make a match near-certain. A cluster fits a generation but not the
expected couple.

Misattributed parentage is one explanation among several. Before concluding it, test: sample or account
errors and mislabelled kits; errors in the *matches'* trees rather than yours; an undocumented or informal
adoption or fostering; a child raised by grandparents or an aunt and recorded as theirs; a step-parent
recorded as a parent; a name change or alias; and a same-named substitution (section 3).

**The discipline when DNA contradicts a documentary conclusion.**

1. **Re-audit the documentary link first.** Very often the "contradicted" link was never proven,
only assumed. Ask which record asserted it and who informed that record.
2. **Do not delete.** The documentary conclusion and its evidence stay in the file.
3. **Write a dated revision note** giving the prior conclusion, the new evidence and the reasoning,
and carry the revision into every derived chart and report (revision and archiving practice is owned by
`references/documentation-standards.md`).
4. **Distinguish the genetic from the documentary relationship in the output.** Both answer real
questions. "Josiah Cray was raised as, and legally was, the son of Bartholomew Cray; autosomal and Y-DNA
evidence indicates his biological father was a member of the Marchbank family" beats either half alone.
5. **Expect the collapse to propagate upward.** A misattributed link invalidates every conclusion
that depended on it, not just the link. List the affected conclusions explicitly.

## 12. Consent, privacy and unexpected findings: a handling protocol

A procedure, not a caveat. Run it before testing, not after a discovery.

**Before testing.** Obtain and record **informed consent** from each tester: what the test can reveal,
that it can reveal unexpected parentage, how results will be shared, how long data are kept, how consent
can be withdrawn. Keep the record with the research file. Consent for someone who cannot give it (a minor,
a person lacking capacity) comes from the person legally entitled to give it, and the reason is
documented. Remember that a tester's result exposes information about **relatives who did not consent**;
weigh that before testing and before publishing.

**While working.** Identify living people by initials, by a described relationship ("a documented
great-grandson of Bartholomew Cray") or by a code, unless they have agreed in writing to be named, and
apply this to match names taken from a testing site as well. Do not publish raw data, match lists or
segment data that identify living people. Follow each platform's terms on what may be exported, shared or
re-uploaded.

**When an unexpected-parentage finding surfaces.**

1. Stop and verify before telling anyone: rule out a tree error, a kit mix-up, a same-name problem.
2. Tell the **test-taker first**, privately, and before anyone else, including whoever commissioned
the research. It is their information about their own body.
3. Give them control of the next step, including the option that nothing is published.
4. Consider who else a disclosure reaches: a living parent, siblings who share a presumption, a
family that does not know. Sequence any disclosure deliberately.
5. Do not publish the identity of a living biological parent or child without consent.
6. For client work, the engagement terms should already say how such a finding is handled. If they
do not, pause and renegotiate rather than proceed.

## 13. Writing relationships in prose

A reader should never have to hold a chart in their head. Four conventions do most of the work.

1. **Introduce a person with dates on first mention**, then use the short form: "Bartholomew Cray
(1788-1851)" first, "Bartholomew" after. Mark estimated dates: "(ca. 1788-1851)". Date conventions are
owned by `references/timeline-building.md`.
2. **Give every same-named person a consistent tag and use it every time.** Take the tag from the
records: a residence ("Bartholomew Cray of Bellwether Creek"), a trade ("the cooper"), or a numbering
reference ("no. 3 below"). Do **not** disambiguate with Jr and Sr unless the records do, and if they do,
note that the labels are relative and can move (section 4). Declare the scheme once: "Three men of this
name appear below and are distinguished throughout as of Bellwether Creek, of Slaughter Neck, and the
younger."
3. **State the path, not just the label.** "Josiah Teale was Bartholomew Cray's second cousin twice
removed, descending through Cray's son Bartholomew the younger" beats "Josiah was a second cousin twice
removed." Labels compress; paths do not.
4. **Anchor everything to one subject** and describe others by their relationship to that person,
rather than switching reference points mid-paragraph.

Two supporting habits: qualify half, step, adoptive and fostered relationships every single time (section
6), and quote a record's own kinship word before giving your reading of it (section 4).

## 14. Failure modes

| Failure | Why it happens | Corrective |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared surname assumed to mean kinship** | Surnames feel like family membership | A surname is a name, not a pedigree; many arose independently from a trade, a place or a patronymic. Require an evidenced link, and for patrilineal questions consider Y-DNA |
| **"Jr" read as "son of"** | Modern convention projected backward | In most historical records Jr and Sr are community-relative age markers that can mark uncle and nephew, cousins or unrelated men, and they move when the elder dies (section 4) |
| **Two same-named men merged** | A name match treated as an identity | Run the identity criteria (2.1) and the separation method (3); test for physical impossibility on a timeline |
| **One man split into two** | Over-caution, or a spelling variant treated as a second person | The same criteria in the other direction; follow the land parcel and the FAN group |
| **An online tree's link accepted** | Fast, and repeated in many trees | A tree is an authored narrative and repetition is not corroboration: a hundred copies of one error is one error. Use the tree to find the *source*, then evaluate the source |
| **A cM figure treated as a relationship** | Tools print a relationship label beside a number | The number maps to a *range* of possible relationships. Report the range, then eliminate alternatives with documents (10.2) |
| **The wife's family ignored** | Records are indexed under the husband | Half of every pedigree runs through women. Search her birth surname in deeds, probate, bonds and church records; married women appear constantly in their fathers' and brothers' files |
| **Eldest son assumed to be named first in a will** | Birth-order intuition | Wills list legatees in whatever order suited the testator or scrivener: by bequest type, by need, by residence, by sex. Birth order comes from evidence, not sequence |
| **Guardianship read as kinship** | Guardians often are relatives | Guardian is an office. Note the appointment, then prove the relationship separately |
| **"Orphan" read as both parents dead** | Modern meaning | In many jurisdictions it means fatherless; look for the living mother in the same file |
| **Sponsor or witness read as kin** | Sponsors often are kin | Establish the community's custom first, then treat the link as a research pointer, not a conclusion |
| **A relationship word modernised silently** | Reading past the word | Log the record's word verbatim, then log your reading and its basis separately (section 4) |
| **DNA used to confirm what was already assumed** | Confirmation bias applied to a new tool | Frame the hypothesis and its alternatives *before* looking at matches, and record which alternatives the DNA could have falsified |

## 15. Self-check before you call a relationship established

- [ ] Every person in the conclusion is individuated, and any same-name separation is written up.
- [ ] Every relationship assertion is traced to a record, and each **informant** is named or
      explicitly noted as unknown, with knowledge and motive assessed.
- [ ] Kinship vocabulary is checked against era and place, and each record's own word is quoted
      before it is interpreted.
- [ ] Half, step, adoptive and fostered relationships are qualified everywhere they appear.
- [ ] Conflicting evidence is resolved in writing, not omitted.
- [ ] Any negative evidence (a child absent from a will, an expected DNA match absent) has had its
      innocent explanations eliminated individually, in writing.
- [ ] DNA evidence, if used, is correlated with the documentary framework, reported as a range of
      possible relationships, and cited; consent and privacy handling is documented.
- [ ] Charts and numbering declare their system, and the prose states relationship paths rather
      than relying on the reader to hold a chart in their head.

are in `templates/kinship-notation.md`.
