# Research Workflows

The loop that turns a vague family curiosity into a defensible conclusion: framing the question,
planning the search, running it, logging every attempt including the failures, and knowing when you
are allowed to stop. This file owns the *process*; its siblings own the artifacts it produces.

**When to load me:** you are about to start or restart research on a person or problem; you are
stuck and cannot tell whether you are stuck or finished; you need a research plan or log; you are
deciding whether a settled conclusion has to be reopened.

**Not here:** citation anatomy and templates (`references/citation-management.md`), chronology and
calendar mechanics (`references/timeline-building.md`), kinship and same-name separation
(`references/relationship-mapping.md`), the proof triad, research reports, and the GPS self-audit
(`references/documentation-standards.md`).

## 0. A note on citing the standards

BCG's *Genealogy Standards* (2nd ed. rev., 2021) renumbered the standards relative to the 2014 first
edition, and BCG publishes a concordance mapping the two schemes. Because a wrong number is worse
than no number, **this file names standards by short title and chapter family** (for example
*Effective research questions*, in the planning-research chapter). Do the same, and quote a standard
only from the book in front of you. The five components of the Genealogical Proof Standard are
stable and safe to name in full: reasonably exhaustive research; complete and accurate source
citations; thorough analysis and correlation; resolution of conflicting evidence; a soundly
reasoned, coherently written conclusion. This workflow makes the first, third, and fourth achievable
and leaves an audit trail the fifth is written from.

## 1. The research cycle

The FamilySearch Research Wiki frames the process as five steps (its pages *Principles of Family
History Research*, *Research Process*, and *A Guide to Research*): **1** identify what you know,
**2** decide what you want to learn, **3** select records to search, **4** obtain and search them,
**5** evaluate and record the results. It is a **cycle**, not a checklist. Step 5 almost never ends
the work; it re-enters the loop at a step determined by *what kind* of result you got. The
discipline is in the re-entry rules, not in the five boxes.

### Where each BCG standard family bites

| Cycle step | What you actually produce | Standards section (ch. 3, *Standards for Researching*, except row 1) | Named standards most in play |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify what you know | A known-facts inventory: every assertion with the source it rests on and its source/information/evidence classification | *Standards for Documenting* (a chapter in its own right, not a section of ch. 3) | *Citation uses*, *Citation elements*, *Separation safeguards* (keep source, information, evidence, and conclusion visibly distinct) |
| 2. Decide what to learn | One focused research question, plus a hypothesis and its falsification condition | Planning Research | *Effective research questions*, *Sound basis*, *Planned research* |
| 3. Select records | A prioritised record-set list with expected yield, access route, and cost | Planning Research | *Broad context*, *Source-based content*, *Topical breadth*, *Efficient sequence*, *Extent* |
| 4. Obtain and search | Images, transcripts, abstracts, and log rows including nil results | Collecting Data | Data-collection scope, careful handling of originals, respect for record custodians, sound use of others' work, reading the handwriting, understanding the terms, note-taking content; plus *Flexibility* from planning |
| 5. Evaluate and record | Classified information, correlated evidence, resolved or flagged conflicts, an updated question set | Reasoning from Evidence (then back to Documenting) | Source/information/evidence classification, analysis, correlation, conflict resolution |

Planning Research, Collecting Data, and Reasoning from Evidence are **sections inside the
researching chapter**, not chapters; only Documenting is its own chapter. *Terminating the plan* is
a Planning Research standard even though you invoke it at step 5: you are deciding whether the
*plan* is discharged, a planning judgement made with step 5's results in hand. *Reasonably
exhaustive research* is not a step either, but a property of steps 3 and 4 together, judged at step
5, and it is what the *Extent* standard is about.

### Re-entry conditions

Do not restart at step 1 by reflex. Re-enter where the result sends you.

| What happened at step 4 or 5 | Re-enter at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered cleanly, and the answer raises the next question | **2** | The inventory is still sound; you only need a new question |
| A finding contradicts something in your known-facts inventory | **1** | The inventory is now wrong. Rebuild it before planning anything |
| The record set turned out not to exist, to be lost, or not to cover your place/date | **3** | The question is fine; the record selection was wrong |
| The record is illegible, damaged, or ambiguous on the point at issue | **4** then **3** | Try a better copy or another version of the same record (bishop's transcript vs parish register, record copy vs signed original) before abandoning it |
| You cannot name any record type that would answer the question as asked | **2** | The question is unanswerable as framed. Reframe it, usually by narrowing the place or widening the date range |
| You cannot state a distinguishing identifier for your subject | **1** | You have an identity problem, not a relationship problem. Solve identity first; see `references/relationship-mapping.md` |
| Two records conflict | **2**, with a new question whose only job is the conflict | Conflicts are their own research problems and deserve their own question |
| A re-search trigger fires (section 9) | **1** | Assume the inventory has aged |

## 2. Framing a focused research question

A question that cannot fail cannot be researched. The planning standards call for *effective
research questions*; an effective one names four things: **a person with a distinguishing
identifier** (not a name, but a name plus what separates this bearer from every other in that place
and period: a dated event, a parcel, an occupation and address, a spouse, a military unit, a run of
tax assessments); **a place**, at the jurisdictional level the records were kept at (4.3); **a time
window** bounded at both ends and defensible from what you know; and **the thing to be
established**, stated so that one finding could establish it and another refute it.

### The three types, bad to good

| Type | Establishes | Bad | Better but still unresearchable | Good |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Identity** | That two or more records refer to the same person, or that they do not | "Who was Bartholomew Cray?" | "Was Bartholomew Cray of Pennsylvania the same man as the Bartholomew Cray of Virginia?" (name only; states are not record-keeping jurisdictions for most of what you need; no window) | "Was the Bartholomew Cray assessed in Cumberland Township, Greene County, Pennsylvania, on the county tax duplicates for 1804 through 1812, the same man as the Bartholomew Crea who, with wife Mary, conveyed land in Washington County, Pennsylvania, in 1791?" |
| **Relationship** | A kinship link between two identified people | "Find Susannah Teale's parents." | "Who were the parents of Susannah Teale of Greene County, Pennsylvania?" (there may be four of her; no window) | "Who were the parents of the Susannah Teale who married Bartholomew Cray on 3 June 1816 in Greene County, Pennsylvania, and who was enumerated in Cumberland Township in 1850 aged 60?" |
| **Activity / event** | That an identified person did a thing, was somewhere, or held a status at a time | "Did the Crays fight in the Revolution?" | "Was Bartholomew Cray a Revolutionary War soldier?" (which one; and a pension file, a militia return, and a bounty-land warrant sit in three different jurisdictions) | "Did the Bartholomew Cray assessed in Cumberland Township, Greene County, Pennsylvania, 1804 through 1812, render military service between 1775 and 1783, and if so in which unit and under which state's or the Continental establishment?" |

Most brick walls are identity questions wearing a relationship question's clothes. If you cannot pin
down *which* Bartholomew Cray you mean, no amount of searching for his parents converges: you are
merging two men's records, and the result will not reconcile.

### Why a vague question produces unfalsifiable research

"Where did the Crays come from?" has no wrong answer, so every finding confirms it and nothing
refutes it. You will accumulate Crays from six counties, none demonstrably yours, and the pile will
feel like progress. Three tests kill vague questions before they waste a month. **The record test:**
name one record type that, if found and legible, would answer this question; if you cannot, it is a
wish. **The refutation test:** name one finding that would force the answer "no"; if nothing could,
the question is unfalsifiable. **The stop test:** describe the state of the evidence at which you
would call the question closed; if you cannot describe it, you will never reach it. Write all three
answers into the plan. The second is the plan's **falsification condition**.

## 3. The research plan

One question per plan; a plan covering "the Cray family" is a wish list. Six parts; the copy-paste
form is `templates/research-plan.md`.

| Part | Contents | The discipline it enforces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Research question | The four-component form above | *Effective research questions* |
| 2. Working hypothesis | A claim that could be wrong, with the reasoning that makes it worth testing before the other candidates. Labelled a hypothesis, and never allowed to migrate into the tree as a conclusion | Keeps the conclusions layer clean |
| 3. Known facts with sources | Every assertion you treat as settled, with a citation and a classification: source type (original / derivative / authored narrative), information type *for that assertion* (primary / secondary / undetermined), and how it bears on the question (direct / indirect / negative evidence) | *Sound basis*. An unsourced assertion is not a known fact; it is an item in part 6 |
| 4. Record sets in priority order | For each: jurisdiction and exact date coverage; repository and access route (online indexed, images only, microfilm, on-site, correspondence); estimated cost in time or money | *Efficient sequence*, *Topical breadth* |
| 5. Expected yield per set | What it tells you if it names your subject, **and** what it tells you if it does not | Makes informative nils worth buying early |
| 6. Falsification condition | What finding kills the hypothesis, plus which part-3 assertions you trust least | *Sound basis*, and the refutation test |

**Priority order** is *Efficient sequence* in practice: expected information gain per unit cost,
with three queue-jumpers. Records at risk (a deteriorating original, a closing repository, an aging
informant); cheap records that would reframe everything (the jurisdictional survey, a published
county record inventory); and records that would resolve an identity ambiguity, because until
identity is settled everything downstream is provisional. **Flexibility** is also a standard: when
step 4 hands you something unexpected, amend the plan in writing and date the amendment rather than
ignoring the finding or silently drifting.

## 4. Reasonably exhaustive research

### 4.1 What the standard demands, and what it does not

**It does not mean every record everywhere:** not exhausting a repository, not searching records
that could not plausibly be relevant, not continuing after the answer is established and
independently corroborated.

**It does mean** every *source class* reasonably likely to hold information relevant to *this
question* about *this person* in *this place and period* has been searched, or shown unavailable,
lost, or out of coverage, with that shown-ness written down. The test is exhaustive-by-class, not
exhaustive-by-item: you do not read every deed in the county, you search the grantor and grantee
indexes for the whole relevant span, and where an index is known incomplete you say so and read the
books for the critical years. It also means you did not stop at *an* answer. Reasonably exhaustive
research is the mechanism by which conflicting evidence gets discovered at all; a conclusion that
has not survived the search for its own counter-evidence is not a conclusion. Four practices carry
the weight: whole-family research, FAN/cluster research, the jurisdictional survey, and the
record-type inventory.

### 4.2 Whole-family and FAN/cluster research

**Whole family** means every child, not just your line; both spouses and their prior and later
marriages; the household in every surviving census year. The younger children's records are often
better than the eldest's, because civil registration caught up with them, and a daughter's marriage
record may name a father the son's does not.

**FAN** is Elizabeth Shown Mills's term, **Friends, Associates, and Neighbours** (the "FAN club"),
also called cluster or collateral research. Your ancestor's paper trail is thin; the trail of the
people around them is not, and those people moved, witnessed, married, and litigated in patterns
your ancestor shares. Harvest the cluster from every document you hold: witnesses to deeds, wills,
and marriages; bondsmen and sureties; executors, administrators, guardians, and appraisers;
grantors, grantees, and the adjoining owners in the metes-and-bounds description; chain carriers on
a survey; jurors and petitioners on a road or court order; godparents, sponsors, and the officiating
minister; informants on death certificates; census, tax-list, and plat neighbours; people in both
the origin and the destination records; fellow soldiers; fellow members of a congregation, lodge, or
benefit society. Then **search the cluster as though each member were your subject**: you are not
looking for them, you are looking for your subject to surface in their records (5.6).

### 4.3 The jurisdictional survey

Before selecting any record set, establish which level of government held which records, in that
exact place, at that exact time, and what is gone. Getting this wrong is the commonest reason a
search returns nothing: the record exists, in a building you did not think to look in.

| Level (US) | Typically held | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Census, military service and pension, bounty land, naturalisation (some), federal land patents, passenger arrivals | Coverage start dates vary by series; 1890 federal population schedules were largely destroyed after a 1921 fire |
| State | Vital registration (start dates vary widely by state), state censuses, militia, state land grants, penitentiary and asylum | Statewide vital registration usually starts far later than people assume, and compliance lags the law by years |
| County | Deeds, wills and probate, orphans' court, marriage (start date varies), tax duplicates, civil and criminal court, coroner | The county that holds the record is the county that existed **then**, not now |
| Town / township / municipality | Town meeting and vital records (strong in New England), assessment, poor relief, cemetery, school | In New England the town, not the county, is the primary record-keeping unit |
| Non-governmental | Church registers, cemetery and burial-ground records, newspapers, funeral homes, fraternal and benefit societies, employers, schools, hospitals, land and immigration companies | Custody may have moved to a denominational archive, a historical society, or a university |

**Non-US analogues.** England and Wales: parish, hundred, poor law union and registration district,
county (records now usually in the county record office), The National Archives. Civil registration
of births, marriages, and deaths begins 1 July 1837; before that the parish register and its
bishop's transcript are the vital records. Ireland: townland, civil parish, barony, poor law union,
county. The 1922 destruction of the Public Record Office of Ireland at the Four Courts removed a
large body of pre-1922 census, probate, and parish material, so the work leans on substitutes
(Griffith's Valuation, tithe applotment books, estate papers, registry of deeds memorials).

**Boundaries and losses.** For US counties, check place and date against the Newberry Library's
*Atlas of Historical County Boundaries*: a family that "moved" often did not, the line moved across
them, and the earlier records stayed with the parent county. For any place the FamilySearch Research
Wiki's place page is the starting inventory; read its record-type list and its record-loss notes
before planning. Record the losses in the plan as specifically as the survivals, by year and series,
with the substitutes. "Burned county" is not permission to stop, it is an instruction to switch to
substitutes (tax, land, church, newspaper, federal and neighbouring-county records, and abstracts
published before the loss).

### 4.4 Record-type inventory by era and place

Build this per place and period. Deliberately half a list: a prompt against forgetting a class.

| Class | Best at establishing | Era caution (US) |
|---|---|---|
| Vital registration and church registers | Birth, marriage, death, and parentage on the record's own terms; sponsors | Civil start dates vary by state and often by county, early compliance partial; registers run centuries earlier, with scattered custody and Latin, German, Dutch, or Welsh text |
| Census | Household composition, approximate ages, birthplaces, migration timing | Pre-1850 federal schedules name only the head, ages come in brackets; 1890 largely lost |
| Land (deeds, patents, surveys) | Presence, residence, economic standing, family transfers, and (via "of [county]" clauses) migration | **The deed book is the clerk's recorded copy**: an original record of the recording act, a derivative of the signed deed. Classify it derivative unless you examined the instrument. Indexes are often alphabetised by first letter only or on an idiosyncratic county scheme; learn the scheme before concluding a nil |
| Probate and court (wills, administrations, inventories, guardianships, equity, orphans') | Relationships stated by the people best placed to know; the whole family in one file; minors' ages from guardianships | The will book stands on exactly the same footing as the deed book, a record copy and not the signed original; ask for the loose estate packet. Case papers are often separate from the docket and not indexed with it |
| Tax | Annual presence, when a son comes of age, when a man dies or leaves | Coverage and content vary year to year; personal and real property lists differ |
| DNA | Biological relationship, which documents assert but cannot independently verify | DNA tests the biology, documents supply the identities. Integrate, never substitute: `references/relationship-mapping.md`. Testing a living person first requires recorded consent (section 8) |

Not tabled, always on the list: military service, pension, and bounty-land files (the application is
usually richer than the service record); immigration and naturalisation (pre-1906 naturalisations
could be filed in almost any court); newspapers (browse the issue, not the search box);
institutional and occupational records, often uncatalogued or restricted.

### 4.5 Searching an index versus searching the records

Different acts, and the log must distinguish them. **Searching an index** tests whether a
name-string an indexer produced matches a name-string you supplied: a search of a derivative source
whose creator never saw your question. **Searching the records** means reading the record itself,
the whole register page, the whole township's tax list, the whole grantor book for the span, the
whole burial ground. A failed index search licenses one statement only, "this index, searched these
ways, did not return this name." It does not license "he is not in this record set." Only a record
search with stated coverage licenses that, and then only so far as the records are complete and
legible. When the question matters and the index is thin, budget for reading the records; the
*Extent* standard is where that budget comes from.

## 5. Search strategy mechanics

### 5.1 Name variants

Spelling was not standardised and your ancestor usually did not control it: the clerk heard a sound
and wrote what he wrote. Generate variants systematically, not by inspiration.

| Source of variation | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Phonetic** | Soundex (crude, but built into some indexes), Daitch-Mokotoff (better for Slavic, German, and Jewish surnames), NYSIIS. Generate candidates with them; never decide identity with them. Cray / Crea / Creigh / Kray / Craigh / McCrea is one practical neighbourhood |
| **Clerical corruption in the hand of the period** | Minims collide in secretary and early round hands (m / n / u / i / w), long s reads as f, doubled f stands for capital F, capital L / S / T confuse, thorn survives as y in "ye". Calibrate on the clerk's *other* entries on the same page before deciding what he wrote |
| **Index corruption** | OCR and handwriting-recognition errors, keying errors, column misalignment attaching the right name to the wrong age or page |
| **Translation and Anglicisation** | Schmidt to Smith, Zimmermann to Carpenter, Guilielmus to William. Latin forms are the norm in many Catholic and some Anglican registers |
| **Naming systems** | Scandinavian -sen / -datter changes every generation; Welsh ap / verch; Iberian double surnames; Russian patronymics. A surname search is the wrong instrument here |
| **Given-name diminutives** | Polly for Mary, Sally for Sarah, Nabby for Abigail, Patsy for Martha. Search the diminutive as a given name in its own right; that is how it was indexed |

Keep the list in the plan and log **which variants you actually tried**, per search. A nil that does
not say which spellings were attempted is worth nothing later.

### 5.2 Wildcards and their limits

Wildcards operate on the index string; they cannot recover what the indexer misread, and they do not
reach browse-only images at all. Syntax and minimum-character rules are engine-specific, most
requiring two or three leading characters and many rejecting a leading wildcard. Run wildcard *and*
exact searches: they often return different, non-nested sets, because exact search may invoke a
fuzzy expansion that wildcard search disables. Truncation (`Cr*`) helps with suffix variation and a
single-character wildcard (`Cr?a`) with a known ambiguous letter; neither helps when the first
letter is wrong, the failure case for names beginning with a vowel, an H, or a silent consonant.

### 5.3 Indexed, image-only, browse-only, and offline

Before you record a nil result, know which of these you searched, because the sentence you are
entitled to write differs.

| Collection state | What a nil result means | What to do next |
|---|---|---|
| Indexed, images linked | The index did not match your strings | Try variants, wildcards, non-name fields, then read the records |
| Indexed, no images | The index did not match, and you cannot verify any hit against the original | Treat every hit as a derivative; obtain the original before relying on it |
| Images online, not indexed (browsable) | Nothing, until you have actually browsed the relevant pages | Browse by volume and date range; log the exact page span you read |
| Browse-only with a paper finding aid | Nothing, until the finding aid is used | Get the finding aid (published inventory, calendar, card index) and log which one |
| Not digitised (film, fiche, paper) | Nothing at all | Order the film, visit, or commission a look-up; log the request and its date |
| Access-restricted | Nothing | Log the restriction and the conditions under which access is possible |

### 5.4 Why an index miss is not a record absence

Before writing "not found", rule out each of these, and note in the log which you checked:

1. The name was indexed under a spelling you did not try.
2. The name was mis-keyed or mis-OCR'd beyond any variant you would guess.
3. The record is in the collection but the page was skipped, filmed out of order, or filmed
   illegibly.
4. The index covers only part of the collection's date range, or only some counties or volumes.
5. Your date window is wrong, because the event was recorded late, or the record is dated by filing
   rather than by event.
6. Your place is wrong, because the jurisdiction changed (section 4.3).
7. The person is there under a different name form: married name, anglicised name, religious name,
   alias, step-parent's surname, initials only.
8. The search interface silently restricted you, by default date range, default collection, or an
   exact-match toggle you did not notice.
9. The record was never created, because the law did not require it then, or the family did not
   comply.
10. The record was created and later lost.

Only 9 and 10 are statements about the world. The other eight are statements about your search.

### 5.5 Searching by something other than the name

When a name search fails, stop searching by name. Search by **spouse, parent, or child** with the
target's surname blank; by **place plus date range with no surname**, reading the whole result set,
where the place is small enough; by **age plus birthplace** with only a given name; by **occupation,
unit, enumeration district, ship, parcel description, or case number**; by the **informant** on a
death certificate, the **witness** on a marriage, the **bondsman** on a bond; in land records, by
the **adjoining owner** in the boundary description.

### 5.6 Reverse and associative searching, and searching the neighbours

Instead of searching for your subject, search a cluster member and read every document they
generated, looking for your subject inside it. This is how people with no records of their own
(women, minors, tenants, the enslaved, the landless) become visible: a woman with no deed appears
releasing dower in her husband's, a minor in his guardian's accounting. Take the cluster list from
4.2, run the record-type inventory for the top five names, and log each search against the
*original* question so the log stays coherent.

"Search the neighbours" is concrete: read the two census pages before and after, noting every
household head, because 1850 neighbours are often kin, in-laws, or fellow migrants from one origin
parish; read the whole township tax list for consecutive years rather than looking up the surname,
so you see who arrives and who vanishes; plat the parcel, identify the adjoiners, search the
adjoiners; record the whole burial-ground section, not the one stone, since adjacent plots were
bought together; read the entries surrounding a parish-register entry, because the same sponsors
recur across a kin group.

## 6. Negative searches and negative evidence

**Not the same thing, and the distinction is load-bearing.**

- A **negative search result** is a fact about your search: you looked, and did not find. No
  evidentiary force on its own.
- **Negative evidence** is an inference from an absence *where presence was expected*, and is one of
  the three kinds of evidence alongside direct and indirect.

Negative evidence is a nil result plus an argument. The argument must establish four things; if one
fails you have only a nil result:

1. **The record set would have covered this person**, given the law, the jurisdiction, and the
   period. (Would he have been taxed? Was registration compulsory and enforced?)
2. **The record set is complete for the relevant span**, or its gaps are known and do not include
   your window.
3. **You searched it properly and exhaustively**, by name variants and, where the index is suspect,
   by reading the records.
4. **The absence is more probably explained by the person's absence than by any of the ten reasons
   in section 5.4.**

Worked contrast, same search, two different statements:

> **Nil result.** The Greene County, Pennsylvania, tax duplicates for Cumberland Township, 1813
> through 1820 (all eight years present and legible), were read line by line; no Cray under any of
> nine spelling variants.
>
> **Negative evidence.** The Cumberland Township duplicates survive complete for 1813 through 1820
> and assess every taxable inhabitant, single freemen included; Bartholomew Cray was assessed in
> this same township in each year 1804 through 1812; the eight duplicates were read line by line,
> every surname, with no index between the reader and the page. On those premises **his absence from
> those lists is evidence that he was not a taxable inhabitant of Cumberland Township in those
> years.**
>
> That he remained in Greene County is *not* carried by the absence. It is carried by the 1816
> marriage (04-06) and the 1834 probate, with which the absence is correlated: the positive records
> place him in the county, the absence says where in the county he was not assessed, and together
> they point at another township as the next search rather than at a conclusion.
>
> **Discharging the four premises.** (1) *Covered*: the same jurisdiction assessed him in each of
> the eight preceding years. (2) *Complete*: all eight duplicates present and legible, no gap. (3)
> *Searched properly*: nine variants, then every surname read with no index between reader and page,
> so reasons 1, 2, 4, and 8 of section 5.4 cannot operate; the duplicates are dated by assessment
> year, not filing, so reason 5 does not bite. (4) *Better explained by his absence*: reasons 9 and
> 10 fail, the series surviving and the law taxing him. **Reason 6 is not excluded, and is not meant
> to be**; it is the limit that sets the scope, which is why the claim is "not a taxable inhabitant
> of Cumberland Township" and not "not in Greene County". **Residual:** a hand no reader would
> connect to Cray, or an alias (reason 7), survives a line-by-line read. That residual is why the
> claim is non-assessment, not non-residence.

The first is a log row; the second an argument, available only because the first was recorded with
its coverage. Note what it does *not* do: it does not smuggle the positive evidence into the
absence, and claims no more territory than the records searched can cover.

### Recording a nil result so it carries weight

Citable only if it records all of the following. Anything less is a note to yourself.

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Source searched, fully cited | Someone else must be able to search the same thing |
| Jurisdiction and exact coverage | "Greene County deeds" is not coverage; "Greene County grantee index, Books 1 through 7, 1796 through 1840" is |
| Completeness and known gaps | Missing volumes, unfilmed years, damaged pages, partial indexing |
| Finding aid used, by name | Card index, published abstract, online index, original book index, or none |
| Exact search terms and variants tried | Including wildcards and which fields were left blank |
| Legibility and physical condition | "Pages 44 through 51 water-damaged, surnames unreadable" changes what the nil means |
| Whether you searched the index or the records | Section 4.5 |
| Searcher and date | Coverage changes; your own eyes change |

`templates/research-log.md` has a field for each. A nil is cited exactly like a positive result,
because you cite the thing you searched, not the thing you found
(`references/citation-management.md`).

### Why an unrecorded nil search gets re-run

Two costs, one fatal. The cheap one is duplication: in eighteen months you will check the orphans'
court dockets again. The fatal one is that an unrecorded nil **cannot be cited**, and any conclusion
resting on indirect or negative evidence depends on a completeness that cannot now be shown. A
lineage society or portfolio judge who reads "no record found" with no coverage statement treats the
argument as unsupported, and is right to.

## 7. Knowing when to stop

*Terminating the plan* is a standard in its own right. Stopping is a decision you justify, not a
feeling you have. Run all seven tests; failing one means you are not finished.

1. **Answered, not guessed.** The answer states as a conclusion with its evidence, not as the
   likeliest of several unexcluded candidates.
2. **Exhaustive by class.** Every class from the jurisdictional survey and record-type inventory
   searched, or its unavailability documented with specifics.
3. **Independent corroboration.** Each key assertion on two independent, high-quality sources where
   such sources can exist for that place and period; where they cannot, said so explicitly rather
   than leaving the gap silent.
4. **Alternatives excluded.** Every competing candidate identity and interpretation eliminated on
   evidence, not on plausibility.
5. **Conflicts resolved in writing.** "I ignored the outlier" is not resolution: see
   `references/documentation-standards.md`, and `references/timeline-building.md` for detecting
   conflicts by sequence.
6. **Saturation.** Recent searches return only what you hold. Weak alone, because it is also what
   confirmation bias feels like; it counts only alongside tests 2 and 4.
7. **The hostile-reader test.** A knowledgeable reader who wanted the conclusion wrong cannot name a
   record class you skipped, a variant you did not try, or an alternative you left alive.

### The honest alternative: a documented open question

Most research stops before test 1. Normal, and not failure, *provided you record it as an open
question rather than a conclusion*; the failure is the silent promotion of a hypothesis into a tree.
The form is in `templates/research-plan.md` and a filled instance closes section 11. The field that
goes wrong most often is **status**: unresolved, partially supported, or contradicted, never
"probable" unless you can defend the word in writing. Park it in the log's open-question tab and as
a note on the person in whatever holds your tree. An open question you cannot find again is a
conclusion you will eventually invent.

## 8. The research log

The log is the audit trail for reasonably exhaustive research: every search goes in it, hit or miss,
before you move on, as the FamilySearch Research Wiki pages *Research Logs*, *Keeping a Research
Log*, and *Prepare a Research Log* also urge. **One log per research question**, not one per person,
so it reads as the record of an argument; cross-index by person separately.

### Columns (the spreadsheet header is `templates/research-log.md`)

| # | Field | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Search ID | Sequential, so log rows can be cited in a report |
| 2 | Date searched | The date you searched, not the date of the record |
| 3 | **Searcher** | Who ran the search, named. A nil is a claim about a pair of eyes at a date: a reader has to know whether you read the film, or an agent did a look-up for you (the access routes in `templates/research-log.md` include both), and whether the searcher could read the hand and the language |
| 4 | Question ref | Which research question this search serves |
| 5 | Objective | What this specific search was expected to establish |
| 6 | Repository / access | Where, and by what route (on-site, film, online indexed, browse images, look-up by agent) |
| 7 | Source searched | Full citation, or a genuine shortened note pointing to the source list |
| 8 | Coverage searched | Jurisdiction, volumes, exact date span, page span if browsed, and **whether the span is event-dated or filing-dated**: a deed book covering recordings 1796-1840 does not cover the same instruments as one covering executions 1796-1840, and section 5.4's reason 5 lives in this gap |
| 9 | Finding aid | Named index or aid used, or "none, read line by line" |
| 10 | Terms tried | Names, variants, wildcards, non-name fields |
| 11 | Result | Nil / hit / partial / ambiguous / inaccessible |
| 12 | Source type | Original / derivative / authored narrative |
| 13 | Information type | Primary / secondary / undetermined, **per assertion**, not per document |
| 14 | **Informant / how they knew** | Who supplied *this* assertion and from what position: named on the record, constrained by the record type, or undetermined |
| 15 | Evidence relation | Direct / indirect / negative, relative to the question in column 4 |
| 16 | Condition | Legibility, damage, gaps, restriction |
| 17 | Filed as | Where the image, transcript, or abstract lives |
| 18 | Analysis and next action | What it means, what it forces, what to do next |

Columns 12 through 15 are the ones people skip and the ones that make the log worth keeping, and
**column 14 is what makes column 13 defensible**. A death certificate is an original source; primary
for the fact and date of death, which the attending physician certified; secondary for the
decedent's parents' names, which the informant, a son-in-law, reported at second hand. One document,
two classifications, because two people supplied the two assertions. Pinning an informant down, and
saying "undetermined" properly when the record names none, is
`references/documentation-standards.md` §1.2, which makes identifying the informant in writing a
prerequisite for classifying at all. Column 14 is where that writing goes. Citation syntax for
column 7 is `references/citation-management.md` (`{curly braces}` are slots, `_underscores_` mark
italics); the one rule this file imposes is that the cell carry the full provenance chain when the
item came through a website, so the layer you saw is visible. Shape only:

```
"{exact database title as the site gives it}," database with images, _{Site Name}_
  ({site root URL} : accessed 14 August 2026), entry for Josiah Cray and Rachel Teale, 1889;
  citing Greene County Marriage Record, vol. 3: 118, Clerk of Orphans' Court, Waynesburg,
  Pennsylvania, and {the film or digital-folder identifier the site names}.
```

Three layers in one sentence: the titled database you searched, the original the clerk wrote, and
the imaging between them. **`citing` marks the boundary of your own knowledge**, so the film or
image-set identifier belongs after it, with everything else the provider asserts and you did not
verify. There is no "imaging" connector; that identifier is not a layer you examined. Drop it and a
reader cannot tell whether you saw an image or a transcription.

### Log hygiene

- Write the row **before** the next search, not at the end of the session.
- A row with an empty coverage cell is not a row. Fill it or delete it.
- Never delete a row for irrelevance. Irrelevance is a finding, and it is what stops you re-running
  the search.
- One unambiguous date format throughout (ISO, or the day-month-year form used in genealogical
  writing); transcribe a record's own date as written and normalise separately. Calendar handling
  and double dating are `references/timeline-building.md`.
- Keep the log where the research report can reach it; archiving and file naming are
  `references/documentation-standards.md`.

### Ethics and privacy in the log

A working record, not a publication, but it is what you hand to a successor and it fills with living
people. Three rules; the full protocol, with the consent register and misattributed parentage, is
`references/documentation-standards.md` §9.

- **Living people.** Log only what the question needs. Identifying detail stays in the private
  archive; in anything circulated the default is name withheld.
- **Restricted content.** Log the restriction, its authority, and the conditions of access, and cite
  the record normally. Where the terms forbid reproduction, log the citation and your finding, not a
  transcript.
- **DNA.** Nobody is tested, or asked to test, without informed consent recorded with a date: what a
  result may reveal, who will see it, what becomes of the sample. A living descendant is a person,
  not a record set, and consent to test is not consent to be named.

## 9. Re-search triggers

A conclusion is a statement about the evidence available when you wrote it. These events invalidate
that "available"; when one fires, re-enter at step 1.

| Trigger | What it threatens | First action |
|---|---|---|
| **A new collection comes online**, or an existing one is re-indexed or newly image-linked | Every nil result against that collection, and every conclusion resting on those nils | Pull the log rows citing that collection or that jurisdiction and re-run them; check whether the new indexing covers years your old search did not |
| **A boundary or jurisdiction discovery**: the county line moved, the parish was divided, the record custody transferred | Everything searched under the wrong jurisdiction, which is usually the whole plan | Redo the jurisdictional survey (4.3), then re-run the record selection; expect the priority order to change |
| **A new DNA match**, or a change in a match's tree or a shared-match cluster | Conclusions about biological relationship, and any identity conclusion the match contradicts or supports | Do not amend the documentary conclusion to fit the match. Frame a new research question about the match, and integrate per `references/relationship-mapping.md` |
| **A conflicting assertion surfaces**, from a new record, a cousin, a published work, or your own later reading | The conclusion, and the completeness of the original search that missed the conflict | Open a research question whose sole object is the conflict. Resolution is required, not optional; see `references/documentation-standards.md` |
| **A record loss is reversed**: a "destroyed" series turns out to survive in a substitute, an abstract, or a private copy | Any negative-evidence argument built on the loss | Re-test the negative-evidence argument's premise 2 (completeness) directly |
| **You learn the handwriting, the language, the law, or the record type better** | Readings and interpretations you made when less competent | Re-read the images you already hold before searching for new ones. This is the cheapest re-search there is |
| **A cited source changes**: a website re-scans, re-attributes, or withdraws a collection; a repository re-catalogues | The citation, and possibly your access to the original | Re-verify the citation and the provenance chain; see `references/citation-management.md` |
| **Time**: five years since the conclusion, and it matters | All of the above, silently | Run the sufficiency tests (section 7) against the current record landscape |

The rule to internalise: **a settled conclusion is reopened by new information about the records,
not by new information about your preferences.** Wanting a different answer is not a trigger.

## 10. Failure modes

| Pathology | What it looks like | Why it fails | The corrective |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Name collecting** | Adding every same-surname person in the county to the tree because the name matches | Name is not identity. In a county with three Bartholomew Crays you have built a chimera | Require a distinguishing identifier before any record is attached (section 2). Separate identities before merging them: `references/relationship-mapping.md` |
| **Tree copying** | Importing an online tree's parents, dates, and places wholesale | You have inherited someone else's unexamined conclusions and their errors, and you cannot cite them | Treat any tree as an authored narrative and a finding aid, never a source. Harvest its *sources*, search those yourself, and cite what you saw |
| **Conclusion drift** | A hypothesis appears in the tree without its "hypothesis" label and hardens over months | Later work is built on it and cannot be unpicked | Keep hypotheses in the plan and open questions in the open-question register (section 7), never in the conclusions layer |
| **Jurisdiction blindness** | Searching the modern county, the modern parish, the modern country | The record is in the parent jurisdiction and you will conclude it does not exist | Jurisdictional survey before record selection, every time (section 4.3) |
| **Chasing the interesting** | Abandoning the plan for a shiny collateral discovery | The original question never gets answered and the log becomes incoherent | Amend the plan in writing or open a new question with its own plan. *Flexibility* means amending, not drifting |

The rest are treated where they belong rather than twice: index-only research at 4.5 and 5.4;
confirmation bias, single-source conclusions, and stopping at the first match at section 7 (tests 3,
4, 7); ignoring the cluster at 4.2 and 5.6; unlogged searching at section 8.

## 11. Worked example, end to end

Illustrative people in structurally real records and jurisdictions. Verify every boundary and
record-start date for your own county against the FamilySearch Research Wiki place page and the
Newberry *Atlas of Historical County Boundaries*.

### Pass 1

**Step 1, identify what you know.** Three assertions, each with its source and classification:

| Assertion | Source | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Bartholomew Cray assessed in Cumberland Twp., Greene Co., Pa., 1804-1812 | County tax duplicates, courthouse | Original; primary as to the assessment, the assessor being the informant; undetermined as to anything else |
| Will of Bartholomew Cray proved Greene Co. 1834, naming wife Susannah and children Abner, Levi, Rachel, and Nancy | Register of wills, will book | The will book entry is a **record copy**, hence derivative of the signed will exactly as a deed book is derivative of the signed deed; primary information for the testator's statements about his family, he being the informant |
| "The Crays came from Virginia" | An undocumented online tree | Authored narrative; no information value until its own source is found |

Step 1 has already demoted the Virginia tradition out of the known-facts set.

**Step 2, decide what to learn.** *"Where did the Crays come from?"* fails all three tests in
section 2. Reframed:

> **RQ-04.** Was the Bartholomew Cray assessed in Cumberland Township, Greene County, Pennsylvania,
> 1804 through 1812, the same man as the Bartholomew Crea who, with wife Mary, conveyed land in
> Washington County, Pennsylvania, in 1791?

Hypothesis: one man, who married twice.

**Falsification condition, and how to get it wrong.** The tempting limb, "a record placing a bearer
of the name in both counties in the same year," is worse than worthless. Greene County was erected
out of Washington on 9 February 1796, so the same ground is recorded at Washington before that date
and at Waynesburg after it: one man near the line can appear in both in one year, and a reader
applying the condition would refute a true identity. Name something **one man cannot satisfy**:

> RQ-04's hypothesis dies if (1) two Bartholomew Crays execute instruments before different
> officials on the same date, or a Washington County record names a Bartholomew Cray "Jr." alongside
> a living senior of the name; or (2) a Washington County record shows the 1791 grantor dead before
> 1804.

**Step 3, select records.** The same 1796 erection puts pre-1796 land on this ground at Washington,
not Waynesburg, and Pennsylvania county marriage records generally begin in 1885, so an 1816
marriage must be sought in church records. Priority: Greene deed indexes; Greene tax duplicates
1813-1820 (cheap, and the nil would be informative); Washington deed indexes pre-1796; Washington
tax lists; orphans' court; churches.

**Steps 4 and 5.** Log rows 04-01 through 04-06, section 8. Three things fall out: continuity of
land (an 1802 Greene purchase and a 1791 Washington sale, adjacent ground); a wife problem (Mary in
1791, Susannah in 1834); and an ambiguity that cannot be used at all, the 1788 Finley Township entry
at 04-04, in territory divided in 1796. **A wife's name is not an identity discriminator**:
remarriage after a wife's death was normal, and the gap between Mary's last appearance and
Susannah's first is long enough to hold both a death and a remarriage, with 04-06 supplying the 1816
marriage. Re-enter at step 2 with a conflict-only question, not at step 3 with more searching.

### Pass 2

> **RQ-05.** Can the Bartholomew Cray of the 1791 Washington County deed and the Bartholomew Cray
> whose will was proved in Greene County in 1834 be one man, given the ages, the land, and the
> associates, and is any third bearer of the name in either county in 1791 through 1834?

**Step 3, cluster-first.** The 1791 deed's grantee and witnesses and the 1802 deed's adjoining
owners become the FAN list, each run through the record-type inventory (5.6). Added: the 1850 census
of Cumberland Township, because Susannah if living will give an age, and the orphans' court dockets
themselves, because 04-05's nil was index-only.

**Step 4 and 5, what the cluster returns.**

- One witness to the 1791 deed appears as an appraiser of the 1834 estate: the same small social
  group brackets both documents, forty-three years apart. Indirect evidence for one man.
- The 1850 census enumerates Susannah Cray, aged 60, widowed. That age is as of 1 June 1850 and
  census ages are routinely rounded, so it yields an interval, not a year: **born c. 1785-1795**.
  An 1816 marriage falls inside it, a consistency flag and not an identification.
- **Lifespan test.** A man conveying land with a wife's dower release in 1791 was of age, so **born
  by 1770** (twenty-one at the conveyance) and at least sixty-four at an 1834 probate; one lifespan
  carries both records. "By 1770" is a boundary and "c. 1790" an estimate, so "by about 1770" merges
  the two into a phrase with no defined meaning. Date arithmetic is
  `references/timeline-building.md`, same-name separation `references/relationship-mapping.md`.
- Orphans' court, now read against the dockets rather than the card index, produces an 1835
  guardianship of **Nancy Cray, aged 11, daughter of Bartholomew, deceased**, the youngest of the
  four children the will names. The index-only nil at 04-05 was wrong: section 4.5 in one row.
- Washington County Book C, read because its index was torn across the target surname (04-03),
  yields no additional Cray. That is now a *record* nil with stated coverage, not an index nil.

**Sufficiency tests.** Tests 1 through 5 pass: identity carried by indirect evidence from land
continuity, associate overlap, and a consistent lifespan; every source class searched or documented
as unavailable; the "two men" reading eliminated; the dower-release conflict resolved by the 1816
marriage. Test 7 finds the gap, the Washington County court order books.

**State the family, or nothing was established.** "Identity established" is not a conclusion until
it names people. One Bartholomew Cray: of Washington County in 1791, conveying with wife Mary; of
Cumberland Township, Greene County, from 1802; married, 3 June 1816, Susannah Teale (04-06); dead in
1834 leaving a will naming Susannah and four children, Abner, Levi, Rachel, and Nancy, the last
still a minor in 1835. Which children are Mary's and which Susannah's is a further question, opened
and not assumed: the will states relationship, not maternity. RQ-05 therefore closes with a proof
summary (the escalation rule is in `references/documentation-standards.md`), while the Virginia
tradition does not close:

```
OPEN QUESTION  OQ-02   opened 2026-03-28   last worked 2026-03-28
Question:            Where was the Bartholomew Cray of the 1791 Washington Co., Pa., deed
                     resident before 1791, and is there evidence of residence in any Virginia
                     county between 1770 and 1791?
Current hypothesis:  Origin in a Virginia county, source unknown
Status:              unresolved. Sole support is an undocumented online tree (authored
                     narrative, no information value). Supports: none of evidentiary weight.
                     Against: none
Missing:             any record naming him outside Pennsylvania before 1791
Next steps:          1. Washington Co., Pa., court order books 1781-1800, for naturalisation,
                        road, or suit entries giving a prior residence
                     2. The 1791 grantee's and witnesses' origins; migrants travel in groups
                     3. Virginia county court order books for the cluster's counties, once its
                        origin is identified
                     4. A targeted DNA test of a documented descendant, informed consent
                        obtained and recorded with a date before a kit is ordered (section 8)
Blocked by:          nothing; cost and time only
Reopen when:         Washington Co. order books are indexed or digitised; a new DNA match in
                     the surname cluster
```

Note the shape of the ending. The question that closed, closed; the one that did not is on the
record as open, with named next steps and repositories, and nothing has been promoted into the tree
that the evidence does not carry.
