# Documentation Standards

How to evaluate what a record is worth, write a conclusion a stranger can test, and keep an archive that
survives you. This file operationalises the Genealogical Proof Standard: it is where "I think she was his
daughter" becomes something a portfolio judge, a lineage society, or a hostile cousin can check.

**When to load me:** you are writing up a conclusion, evaluating a source, resolving a conflict, producing a
research report, deciding whether something is *proved*, naming and filing images, revising a published
conclusion, or handling a finding that touches a living person, DNA, or enslavement.

**Siblings, whose work I do not repeat.** Search strategy, planning, logs, and the "have I searched enough"
judgement: `references/research-workflows.md`. Citation syntax and the layered templates:
`references/citation-management.md`. Dates, calendars, place normalisation: `references/timeline-building.md`.
Kinship, identity resolution, same-name separation, numbering, DNA correlation:
`references/relationship-mapping.md`.

> **Two conventions.** (1) Square brackets, like `[page]` or `[URL]`, mark a slot you fill from the record in
> front of you; this file supplies no number it has not verified and neither should you. A wrong number is
> worse than a visible blank. (2) *Genealogy Standards* (2nd ed. rev., Board for Certification of
> Genealogists) numbers its standards; this file names them by content instead. Read the number off the book
> in your hand before you cite one.

## 1. Source, information, evidence, proof: one analytical sequence

Four stages, not four synonyms, each with its own question. You cannot skip one.

| Stage | The question | Property of |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | What thing am I holding, and how far is it from the event? | the artifact |
| **Information** | Who said this particular thing, and how did they know? | one assertion inside it |
| **Evidence** | What does that assertion do for *my* question? | the assertion-to-question relationship |
| **Proof** | Does the correlated whole carry the conclusion? | the argument, not any item |

The standard amateur failure collapses all four: "it's a good source, so it's true." A death certificate is an
original source carrying primary information about the death and secondary information about the birth,
offering direct evidence of the death date and direct evidence of the parents' names that is only as good as the
secondary information carrying it. All true at once. Weak information does not make evidence indirect; for what
indirect evidence actually looks like, see the census pair in §1.3.

### 1.1 Source: original, derivative, authored narrative

| Class | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Original** | The record as first created, or a faithful image of it | the signed deed, the parish register page, the filed death certificate, a gravestone photograph |
| **Derivative** | Produced by copying, transcribing, abstracting, indexing, translating, or re-recording | deed and will books, typed certified copies, published transcriptions, every index, most databases |
| **Authored narrative** | A work of interpretation built from sources | county histories, compiled genealogies, journal articles, online trees, this file's examples |

**The photocopy trap.** "It came straight from the courthouse" tells you about provenance, not class. A
reproduction is *original* only if the thing reproduced is the record as created. Ask: **has a human re-keyed
anything between the event and this page?** If yes it is derivative, however official the paper.

- **Certified copy vs. image.** A certificate the clerk typed afresh and sealed in 2019 is a derivative. A
  microfilm frame or digital image of the 1921 certificate as filed is an image of an original. The seal
  certifies accuracy; it does not undo the copying.
- **Recorded deeds and wills.** The deed book is an original record of the *recording act* but a derivative of
  the signed deed. Where loose originals survive (US county estate files; in England and Wales, the probate
  registry files behind the printed calendars), they outrank the bound volume.
- **Re-recorded records** after a courthouse fire, sometimes decades later, sometimes from memory.
- **Delayed birth certificates.** An original record of a *later* proceeding, carrying secondary information
  about a birth decades earlier. Evaluate each supporting affidavit's informant separately.
- **Database "records."** An index entry is derivative even when displayed beside the image. Cite what you
  actually examined; if you read only the index, say so.

**Tells that you hold a derivative:** one uniform hand or typeface across entries different people would have
signed; signatures rendered `/s/` or in the clerk's hand; an added attestation clause; missing marginalia,
amendments, and strike-throughs the record type normally carries; pagination not matching the original series;
a database "source" naming a repository other than the one you queried.

An **image** inherits the class of the thing imaged, but adds a layer the citation must show: website layer,
film or image layer, underlying original, in that order (`references/citation-management.md`).

### 1.2 Information: per assertion, driven by the informant

Classification attaches to **one assertion**, never a document. The question is always: was the person who
supplied *this statement* in a position to know it, at first hand, at the time?

- **Primary** — direct personal knowledge, ordinarily recorded at or near the time. The midwife on the birth.
  The bride on her own marriage. The physician on the death he certified.
- **Secondary** — reporting what they were told, remembered long after, or reconstructed. A son on his
  mother's birthplace. Anyone's age on a census.
- **Undetermined** — you cannot identify the informant or their position. Badly underused, and entirely
  respectable. Guessing "primary" because the document looks official is not.

**Identify the informant explicitly, in writing, before classifying.** (1) Does the record name one? Death
certificates, inquests, naturalisation and pension witnesses, marriage bond sureties, and delayed-birth
affidavits usually do. (2) If not, does the record type constrain one? A census enumerator recorded whoever
answered the door, unnamed but positionally constrained; a parish clerk wrote what the party before him said.
(3) Can handwriting or signature separate who *wrote* from who *spoke*? Only the speaker is the informant.
(4) If you get no further: **undetermined**, and say what you tried.

**Unknown informant** is a ceiling, not a disqualification: such an assertion cannot carry a conclusion alone,
but it can corroborate, and it can be promoted later when you find the informant. Record the promotion with a
date and reason (§8).

**One informant, two classifications** is the normal case. The son reporting his mother's death is primary for
her marital status at death, her residence, and his own relationship to her; secondary for her birth date,
birthplace, and parents' names, because he was at none of those events. Splitting a document's assertions this
way is the highest-yield analytical habit in this file.

### 1.3 Evidence: a relationship, not a property

Evidence is what an item **does for a stated question**. Change the question and the classification changes.
Nothing is "direct evidence" in the abstract.

| Kind | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | Answers the question alone, without inference | Could I state the answer by quoting this and nothing else? |
| **Indirect** | Answers it only combined with other items plus an inference | Must I say "therefore" to get from this to the answer? |
| **Negative evidence** | Absence where the records would be expected to show presence, supporting an inference | Would a competent record-keeper have recorded this had it happened? |

One item, two questions: *the 1850 US census lists Susannah Cray, age 46, born North Carolina, in Bartholomew
Cray's household in Rowan County.* For "was she in Rowan County in 1850," direct. For "who was her father,"
indirect: it bears on her birth year and birth state, which bear on which Teale household could have held her
in 1820.

**Negative evidence is not a negative search result**, and the conflation hides sloppy work.

- A **negative search result** is a fact about your research: you searched, the person was not there. It
  belongs in the report's sources-searched section (§5) and the log (`references/research-workflows.md`). It
  supports no inference alone, because the cause may be a gap in the source.
- **Negative evidence** is an inference from absence, available only once you have established that presence
  was to be expected. Write the expectation first or it reads as hand-waving: *"Rowan County recorded guardian
  bonds for the minor heirs of every intestate decedent in this period, and the clerk's bond book for 1838 to
  1841 contains none for the Teale estate; therefore Jesse Teale left no minor children."*
- **The expectation itself needs a citation.** An uncited expectation is an assumption wearing the clothes of
  negative evidence. Cite the statute, the county practice, or two dated examples from the same series showing
  that the record-keeper did record this when it applied (worked example: §3.4, Item E).

### 1.4 Worked evaluation: one death certificate, assertion by assertion

The canonical messy document: a US state death certificate (Pennsylvania, 1921; statewide registration there
from 1906). The non-US analogue is a civil registration death entry (England and Wales GRO from 1837,
Scotland's statutory registers from 1855), which names the informant and their qualification explicitly and is
correspondingly easier to analyse.

**Decedent:** Susannah (Cray) Vardy, a granddaughter of the Bartholomew Cray and Susannah Teale used in §3
(her father, Jesse Cray, at row 10 below, was their son), so the two worked examples sit in one family without
being about the same woman.
**Artifact:** a digital image of the certificate as filed, so an image of an original. Had it been a typed
certified copy, every line below would carry one more copying act between you and the informant.
**Informants:** three, and the form distinguishes them. The attending physician signed the medical section;
the personal section was supplied by "Jesse Vardy, son, 114 Larimer Ave."; the undertaker completed burial.

| # | Assertion | Supplied by | Position relative to the event | Information |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Died 14 March 1921 | physician | attended her last illness; certifies the date | primary |
| 2 | Died at 114 Larimer Ave., Pittsburgh | physician / undertaker | present at or just after death | primary |
| 3 | Cause: chronic myocarditis | physician | eyewitness to the illness | primary **as to observation**; an expert opinion as to cause, and a 1921 diagnosis is not a modern one |
| 4 | Female, white | son | lifelong knowledge | primary |
| 5 | Widowed | son | knew her marital state at death | primary, though he is secondary about the marriage itself |
| 6 | Husband: Ambrose Vardy | son | not present at the marriage | secondary |
| 7 | Born 11 June 1848 | son | not present at the birth | secondary: the usual source of the usual error |
| 8 | Age 72 y 9 m 3 d | derived | arithmetic on #1 and #7 | **not an independent assertion**; it inherits #7's weakness |
| 9 | Birthplace: North Carolina | son | not present | secondary |
| 10 | Father: Jesse Cray | son | reporting family knowledge | secondary; usable, not decisive |
| 11 | Mother's maiden name: blank | son | did not know, or was not asked | **no assertion**; a blank is data about the record, not the mother |
| 12 | Occupation: housewife | son | lifelong knowledge | primary |
| 13 | Informant's name and address | the form | self-reported | primary; this line is what makes the analysis possible |
| 14 | Buried Allegheny Cemetery, 16 March 1921 | undertaker | recorded **prospectively**, before the burial | primary as to intent, undetermined as to the event; verify against the cemetery's own record |
| 15 | Filed 18 March 1921 | registrar | the filing act | primary; dates the record, and so the recency of #1 |

What the same paper is evidence *of*, for three questions:

- *When did she die?* #1: direct evidence, primary information, original source, no conflict. A **proof
  statement** (§3).
- *Who was her father?* #10 is direct in form but secondary information at two removes, recorded 72 years
  after her birth from family tradition. **Direct evidence of weak information is still weak**: a lead to
  test, not a conclusion.
- *Was she a daughter rather than a step-daughter of Jesse Cray?* #10 is silent on the distinction, so here it
  is at best indirect and the question needs other records.

**When the age and the date of birth do not agree.** Row 8 works only because 11 June 1848 to 14 March 1921 is
exactly 72 years 9 months 3 days, so the age is derived and carries no weight of its own. That is the tidy
case and not the common one. When the stated age and the stated date of birth do **not** agree, the age is not
derived at all. It is a second statement by the same informant, and the disagreement is a conflict *inside one
document*, to be stated and resolved under §4 like any other conflict. Check the arithmetic on every
certificate before classifying row 8; that check is the only thing telling you which of the two cases you are
in. Where they disagree, record both as written, say which you prefer and why, and never let software
silently compute a third value. Arithmetic and "on or before" boundaries: `references/timeline-building.md`.

Note what the table refuses to do: it never averages the lines into "a good document." Lines 1 and 7 sit on
one sheet of paper and are worth entirely different amounts.

## 2. The Genealogical Proof Standard, element by element

Five ways for a conclusion to fail. Each has a test a *reader* can apply to your write-up without access to
your files; if they cannot apply it, you have not met the element, however good the research was.

| Element | What must be true | Operational test for the reader | Fails as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reasonably exhaustive research** | You searched what a competent researcher would search for this question in this time and place, **including sources that could disprove you**, and used originals where they survive and are reachable | The write-up names the sources searched, nil results included; a reader who knows the locality cannot immediately name an obvious untouched record group | Conclusions built wholly from indexed databases; every source agreeing because all descend from one original; nothing sought that could have contradicted the answer |
| **Complete and accurate source citations** | Every assertion not common knowledge carries a citation precise enough for a stranger to retrieve the same item, showing the layers it came through | Pick three citations at random: could a stranger retrieve the item from the citation alone? Does an online citation show website, image/film, and underlying original? Does it name what *you* examined? | "Ancestry.com"; "county records"; a bare URL; a citation copied from a database's source block for an image you never opened |
| **Thorough analysis and correlation** | Every item classified (source class, information class per assertion, evidence type for the stated question) and laid beside the others so agreements, gaps, and contradictions are visible | The correlation appears in the write-up as a table, chronology, or walk-through, not only in your head | A list of sources followed by a conclusion; ages never checked against each other; DNA asserted beside documents with no stated relationship |
| **Resolution of conflicting evidence** | Every conflict found is stated and either resolved with reasons or declared unresolved | A reader who independently found the contradicting record finds it already discussed in your text | Omission, majority vote, or picking the source you like (§4) |
| **A soundly reasoned, coherently written conclusion** | The conclusion exists **in writing**, states what is concluded and at what confidence, and runs from evidence to answer in a form a reader can follow and attack | A reader can restate your argument in their own words and name the one item whose removal would most damage it | A populated database with no prose; confidence exceeding evidence; hedging used instead of saying how strong the case is |

Two consequences worth stating flatly. **"Reasonably" is doing real work**: it means proportionate and
defensible, not infinite, and what makes it defensible is that you say what you searched. (When to *stop*
searching is `references/research-workflows.md`; the duty to **document the extent** is here.) And **unwritten
proof is not proof.** A conclusion held only in your head or in software fields has not met the fifth element.
This is the element hobbyists skip, and it is the one that turns research into genealogy.

### 2.1 Self-audit: run before calling anything proved

```
GPS SELF-AUDIT                  Conclusion ID: ____   Date: ____   Auditor: ____

RESEARCH EXTENT
[ ] Research question written down, specific, answerable (person fixed by more than a name;
    place and period fixed).
[ ] Record groups searched listed, with jurisdiction and date span.
[ ] Sources searched with NIL results listed, not only the productive ones.
[ ] At least one source sought that could have DISPROVED the conclusion.
[ ] Originals or images of originals used wherever they survive and are reachable; where only
    a derivative was used, the write-up says so and says why.
[ ] Known record loss and gaps for this jurisdiction and period stated.

[ ] Every assertion carries a citation, or is explicitly flagged as an inference drawn in text.
[ ] Each citation names what I actually examined (index vs. image vs. original).
```

## 3. The proof triad: statement, summary, argument

Three written forms, escalating with the complexity of the *reasoning*, not the importance of the conclusion.
Picking the wrong one is itself a documentation failure: a proof statement under a contested relationship is
an unsupported assertion, and a six-page argument for an uncontested birth date wastes your reader.

| If... | Use |
|---|---|
| Direct evidence, one or two good sources, no conflict, identity not in question | **Proof statement** |
| Direct evidence from several sources all pointing the same way, where the reader benefits from seeing what each says and how far the research reached | **Proof summary** |
| Evidence is indirect; **or** evidence conflicts; **or** negative evidence carries part of the load; **or** identity must be established before the relationship; **or** the answer rests on a chain of inference | **Proof argument** |

Any single trigger in the third row forces a proof argument. You cannot downgrade because the conflict feels
minor.

### 3.1 Proof statement

A sentence or two plus citation. The proof lives in the citation.

> Susannah (Cray) Vardy died 14 March 1921 at 114 Larimer Avenue, Pittsburgh, Allegheny County,
> Pennsylvania.[^1]
>
> [^1]: Pennsylvania, death certificate no. [number] (1921), Susannah Vardy; Division of Vital Records, New
> Castle. Digital image, [website name] ([URL] : accessed [date]); imaged from [film or image series], which
> reproduces the certificate as filed. Certificate examined as an image, not as the paper original; date of
> death certified by the attending physician.

That last clause is the habit to copy: one phrase naming *which* informant carried the assertion turns a bare
citation into an evaluated one.

### 3.2 Proof summary

Several direct-evidence items, all agreeing. Show what each contributes, then state the scope, so the reader
knows the agreement was not achieved by stopping early.

> **Conclusion.** Bartholomew Cray and Susannah Teale married in Rowan County, North Carolina, on or about
> 14 November 1821.
>
> - A Rowan County marriage bond dated 14 November 1821 was issued for them, with Jesse Teale as
>   bondsman.[^2] The bond records the licensing, not the ceremony; North Carolina bonds were ordinarily
>   issued within days of the marriage.
> - The clerk's marriage register records the return for the same couple, with no exact date of ceremony.[^3]
> - The Cray family Bible, in one hand throughout in a volume imprinted 1834, records "married Nov. 1821."[^4]
>   Written at least thirteen years after the event by an unidentified compiler: corroborating, not
>   independent proof of the day.
>
> **Scope.** Rowan County marriage bonds, the clerk's marriage register, and county court minutes for 1820 to
> 1823 were searched. No church register survives for the congregation the Crays are known to have attended.
> No conflicting date was found in any source searched.
>
> [^2]: Rowan County, North Carolina, marriage bonds, bond of Bartholomew Cray and Susannah Teale,
> 14 November 1821, Jesse Teale bondsman; Rowan County records, State Archives of North Carolina, Raleigh.
> [^3]: Rowan County, North Carolina, marriage register [volume]: [page], Cray-Teale return; State Archives of
> North Carolina, Raleigh.
> [^4]: Cray family Bible records, 1821 to 1889, [Bible title and imprint]; privately held by [owner],
> [city, state], 2026. [Owner] received the volume from [prior holder], [relationship to owner], in [year],
> who had it from [prior holder], [relationship], of [place]. Entries in one hand; compiler unidentified;
> title page and the page bearing the entries photographed [date]. The custody chain is not decoration: an
> unprovenanced Bible is an unattributable manuscript.

That "Scope" paragraph is what separates a proof summary from a pile of agreeing citations. It is the
reasonably-exhaustive element made visible.

### 3.3 Proof argument: anatomy

A proof argument is an **adversarial** document. You are making the case against yourself as well as for your
answer.

> **A proof argument that only marshals supporting evidence is not a proof argument.** It is an illustrated
> assertion. The evidence pointing elsewhere, and what you did with it, is not an appendix; it is the part
> worth reading. If your draft contains no sentence beginning "Against this," go back and find what you left
> out.

1. **The question.** One sentence, specific enough to be answerable and to fail.
2. **Background and identity context.** Who this person is; how you know the records you are about to
   correlate are all about *her*; the jurisdiction and its record survival; the state of prior belief.
   Identity precedes relationship, and a relationship argument on unresolved identity collapses. Mechanics:
   `references/relationship-mapping.md`.
3. **The evidence, presented and evaluated.** Per item: what it says, source class, whose information and how
   they knew, what it is evidence *of* for this question, and what it does not establish. Presented, not
   merely cited.
4. **The correlation.** The items laid against each other, usually as a table or chronology. The argument is
   actually made here.
5. **Conflicting evidence, stated and resolved.** Its own heading. Both sides at strength (§4).
6. **The extent of the research.** What was searched, with jurisdiction and span, nil results included, and
   the known record loss for the period. This is the reasonably-exhaustive element made visible; without it
   the reader cannot apply the test in §2. In `templates/proof-argument.md` it is section 8, placed after the
   apparatus so the argument's prose runs unbroken. Either position works; absence does not.
7. **The conclusion.** What, at what confidence, with residual doubt named.
8. **The citation apparatus.** Full reference notes, shortened notes on repetition, and a source list if the
   argument circulates alone. Three shapes: `references/citation-management.md`.

### 3.4 Proof argument: worked example on indirect evidence

---

**Who was the father of Susannah Teale, who married Bartholomew Cray in Rowan County, North Carolina, on
14 November 1821?**

**Background.** Susannah Teale, born in North Carolina about 1804 (the estimate is contested; see the
unresolved conflict in §4, and every inference below that depends on it is marked ‡), married Bartholomew Cray
in Rowan County in 1821, appeared with him there in 1830, 1840, and 1850, and last appears in 1860. North
Carolina required no civil registration of births until 1913 and no baptismal register survives for any Rowan
County congregation covering 1795 to 1810. No record states her parentage in words, so the question must be
answered from indirect evidence, and it turns first on identity: the 1821 bride, the woman in Bartholomew
Cray's household from 1830 to 1860, and the "Susannah Cray" in a Teale estate division must be shown to be one
woman before any parentage inference is available.

Continuity of household, county, husband's name, and stated birth state across all five appearances carries
that identity. A competing candidate was searched for and not found: the Rowan County marriage bonds and the
clerk's marriage register, the federal census population schedules for the county for 1820 through 1860 read
page by page, and the county's estate and guardian records for 1821 through 1845 were searched under Cray,
Crey, and Craig, and no second Susannah Cray of comparable age appears in any of them. That is a **negative
search result with a stated scope**, not negative evidence (§1.3): it eliminates the candidates those record
groups would have held, and no others. Rowan County tax lists for the 1820s were not searched, and a
same-named woman who never married, never held land, and never took a share could still be invisible to all
of the above.

**The evidence.**

*Item A. The marriage bond.* Rowan County, 14 November 1821, for Bartholomew Cray and Susannah Teale, Jesse
Teale bondsman.[^5] Original source; primary information as to the bonding, from the parties themselves. For
the parentage question this is **indirect**: it names no relationship. Its force comes from North Carolina
practice, in which the bondsman was ordinarily a close male relative of the bride, most often father or
brother, standing surety against a legal impediment. Practice is not law, and some bondsmen were friends or
the groom's kin. This narrows the field; it does not close it.

*Item B. The 1820 census.* Jesse Teale, Rowan County, heading a household including one female aged 16 to
25.[^6] Original source; information of undetermined origin, the enumerator's informant being unnamed.
**Indirect**: consistent with a daughter of Susannah's estimated age at home the year before her marriage,
and consistent with several other things.

*Item C. The 1830 census.* Jesse Teale's household in the same county holds no female in the bracket that
would then contain Susannah, while Bartholomew Cray's does.[^7] Original source; information of undetermined
origin, the enumerator's informant being unnamed here as in 1820. **Indirect**, and meaningful only in
sequence with Item B: a female of the right age left Jesse Teale's house in the decade a Susannah Teale left
it to marry. It does not establish that the female who left was Susannah, that she left to marry rather than
to die or move, or that the two enumerations counted the same people accurately; age brackets in these
schedules are the enumerator's arithmetic on a reported age.

*Item D. The estate division.* Jesse Teale died intestate in Rowan County in 1838. The report of division
returned 9 May 1839 divides his estate into seven equal shares, one of them to "Bartholomew Cray in right of
his wife."[^8] Original source, loose estate papers; primary information, recorded by the commissioners who
made the division. **Indirect, and load-bearing.** It uses no relationship word. Under North Carolina
intestacy law of the period an intestate's personal estate passed in equal shares to his children, with the
issue of a deceased child taking that parent's share by representation. Seven equal shares, one claimed by a
husband in his wife's right, is consistent with seven surviving children of whom one was a married daughter,
and equally consistent with a distribution in which a share passed by representation to grandchildren.

A third reading must also be excluded. Under North Carolina's act of distributions a widow took a child's part
where the intestate left more than two children, so seven shares is equally consistent with a widow and six
children. Read the act in force at the date of the division before relying on the fraction: the shares varied
with the number of children, and later acts altered them. Three readings, one record: the count of shares
alone decides nothing.

*Item E. Negative evidence: no representation, and no widow.* Two absences carry part of the load here, and
neither means anything until the expectation behind it is stated and cited.

*The expectation, cited.* Rowan County took guardian bonds for the minor heirs of intestate decedents
throughout this period, and the commissioners returning divisions in this county wrote representation into the
return itself when a deceased child's issue took a share: two returns in the same volume as Jesse Teale's, one
of [date] and one of [date], name the grandchildren and say they take their parent's part.[^9] The expectation
is cited, not assumed, which is the only thing separating the two paragraphs below from hand-waving.

*No representation.* The clerk's guardian bonds for 1838 to 1841 contain none arising from the Teale estate,
and the estate file names no grandchild and uses none of that representation language.[^9] **Negative
evidence**, and it removes the second reading of Item D.

*No widow.* A widow in a Rowan County intestate estate of this period ordinarily leaves traces: a dower
allotment returned by a jury, a year's provision laid off to her, her receipt among the vouchers in the estate
file, and, where she administered, a bond in her own name. The estate file, the county court minutes for 1838
and 1839, and the administration bonds for the same span show none of the four, and administration was granted
to a man the bond describes by no relation to the decedent.[^9] **Negative evidence**, and it removes the
third reading, leaving seven distributees as seven surviving children.

*What it does not establish.* An absence licenses only what its expectation covers. These do not establish
that Jesse Teale never married, that no child predeceased him without issue, or that a widow who herself died
between 1838 and the 1839 division left no trace in some record group not searched. A volume with lost or
unbound leaves produces the same silence as a bond never taken, so the condition of each volume examined is
recorded in the apparatus with the search.

*Item F. The 1850 census.* Susannah Cray, age 46, born North Carolina, in Bartholomew Cray's household.[^10]
Original source; secondary information from an undetermined informant, whoever answered the enumerator.
**Indirect** for parentage; it supplies the census-derived birth estimate of about 1804 ‡ against which the
other items are tested. It does not establish her birth year, which is in unresolved conflict with the family
Bible (§4); it does not name a parent; and because the estimate it supplies is the one contested, every
inference below that turns on her age is marked ‡ and would have to be re-run on a birth in 1799.

**Correlation.**

| Year | Jesse Teale's household | Susannah | Inference available |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1820 | one female 16 to 25 | unmarried, est. age 16 to 21 ‡ | consistent with residence in the household ‡ |
| 1821 | Jesse Teale stands bond | marries Bartholomew Cray | bondsman is close kin of the bride |
| 1830 | that bracket now empty | in Bartholomew Cray's household | a female of that age left in the interval ‡ |
| 1838 | dies intestate | married, living, in county | eligible as a distributee |
| 1839 | estate divided seven ways | husband takes a share in her right | she is a distributee in her own right |

| 1839 | no dower, no year's provision, no widow's receipt | | no widow took a child's part among the seven |

‡ depends on the contested birth estimate of about 1804. On the Bible's 3 February 1799 she is 21 in 1820 and
still inside the 16-to-25 bracket, so the 1820 and 1830 inferences survive either reading; her age at marriage
changes from 17 to 22, which no item in this argument turns on. The mark stays because the dependency is real
and a later reader must be able to see it, not because it breaks the argument. Rule: §4.

No single item names Susannah as Jesse Teale's daughter. In sequence they describe a woman of the right age in
his household, leaving it to marry under his suretyship, and taking a child's equal share of his intestate
estate eighteen years later.

**Scope of research.** Searched in Rowan County, North Carolina: marriage bonds and the clerk's marriage
register, 1815 to 1830; estates and loose estate papers, 1830 to 1845; guardian bonds and administration
bonds, 1830 to 1845; county court minutes, 1836 to 1842; deeds, grantor and grantee indexes, 1795 to 1845;
wills, 1795 to 1845; and the federal census population schedules for the county, 1790 through 1860, read page
by page for 1820, 1830, and 1850. Variants tried throughout: Teale, Teal, Teele, Tale; Cray, Crey, Craig. Nil
results are set out in the report behind this argument and, where one carries part of the load, in Item E.
**Not searched:** county tax lists, and the records of the adjoining counties into which Teales are known to
have moved. **Known loss:** North Carolina required no civil registration of births until 1913; no baptismal
register survives for any Rowan County congregation covering 1795 to 1810; and the guardian-bond volume for
[span] is water-damaged for [leaves], which is stated because an absence found in a damaged volume is a weaker
absence. This paragraph is the reasonably-exhaustive element made visible, and it is what lets a reader apply
the first test in §2 without access to the files.

**Conflicting evidence.**

*The conflict.* A published county history states that Susannah, wife of Bartholomew Cray, was a daughter of
**William** Teale, Jesse's brother.[^11] It is the only source addressing parentage in words, it contradicts
the conclusion above, and it is the version currently repeated in several online trees.

*The case for it, at strength.* It is explicit where all my evidence is inferential. Its author was a Rowan
County resident writing in 1897 with access to informants a century closer to the events than I am, and to
courthouse records some of which no longer survive. Direct evidence, even late, deserves an answer rather
than a wave. It is also true that a bondsman may be an uncle and that an uncle's household could hold a niece
in 1820, so Items A and B are compatible with the county history.

*The case against it.* Three things. First, the county history is an authored narrative citing no source for
the statement, and none of its family sketches carry documentation. Second, William Teale's will, written
2 January 1841 and proved the following month, names five children and devises his entire estate among them;
Susannah is not among them, and the will has no residuary or unnamed-issue clause under which a living married
daughter could be silently passed over.[^12] Third, and decisively, Item D is not compatible with the county
history at all: a distributive share of Jesse Teale's intestate personal estate was available to Jesse's
children and to the issue of his deceased children, not to his brother's daughter. Were Susannah William's
child, she had no claim on Jesse's estate, and the commissioners who awarded her husband a full equal share in
1839 were in error about a family they lived among.

*Resolution.* Resolved against the county history, and not by counting sources but by comparing them. The
county history is an undocumented authored narrative written 76 years after the marriage with no stated
informant. Items D and E are original records created by identified officials with primary knowledge of the
act they recorded and a legal consequence attached to getting it right. Where an undocumented late narrative
contradicts contemporaneous legal records whose makers had reason to be accurate, the narrative yields.
Item F's age of 46 would place Susannah's birth about 1804 and make her 17 at marriage, compatible with the
1820 bracket and with the conclusion.

**Conclusion.** Susannah (Teale) Cray, wife of Bartholomew Cray, was a daughter of Jesse Teale of Rowan
County, North Carolina. The conclusion rests on indirect evidence correlated across four original records and
confirmed by negative evidence, and it survives the one direct statement to the contrary because that
statement is undocumented, late, and inconsistent with the estate division.

**Residual doubt, stated.** The estate division establishes that Susannah took a child's share. It does not by
itself distinguish a natural daughter from a step-daughter or an adopted daughter treated as a child in the
distribution, and Rowan County records disclose no wife for Jesse Teale before about 1795. A deed of gift from
Jesse Teale to Susannah, a Bible record from the Teale line, or an identification of Jesse's wife would settle
it. Until then the conclusion is that Susannah was treated as Jesse Teale's daughter in the eyes of the law and of
the county, which is the sense in which the estate division speaks, and was probably his daughter by birth.
(Period records often use *daughter-in-law* to mean step-daughter; the term is avoided here for that reason.
Kinship-term drift: `references/relationship-mapping.md`.)

## 4. Resolving conflicts in writing

**The discipline.** (1) **State the conflict** under its own heading, in your own words, before resolving it;
the reader must see it from you first. (2) **Present both sides at their strongest.** Steelman the side you
are about to reject, including the parts that genuinely trouble you; a resolution that defeats only a weak
version has resolved nothing. (3) **Weigh by quality, not count.** Six derivative sources copied from one bad
index are one source with five echoes. **Independence is the hidden variable: two sources corroborate only if
they could have disagreed.** (4) **Explain the resolution**, naming the principle, not just the outcome.
(5) **Never delete the losing evidence.** It stays in the write-up, the apparatus, and the archive,
permanently. The next researcher will find it, and if you never mentioned it they must assume you missed it
and re-litigate everything you wrote.

**Resolving principles**, in rough order of force, none automatic, each one you must state and may have to
defend: contemporaneous record over later recollection; first-hand informant over family tradition; original
over derivative, and fewer copying acts over more; a record with legal or financial consequence over one with
none; a record made for a purpose unrelated to the disputed point over one made to establish it; consistency
with an independently established chronology (`references/timeline-building.md`); and a plausible **mechanism
of error** identified.

Identifying the mechanism is the strongest form of resolution. "The 1860 age is wrong" is weak. "The 1860
enumerator recorded every age on this page as a multiple of five, so 45 here means 43 to 47" is strong,
because it *explains* the losing evidence instead of merely overruling it.

**Three honest outcomes.**

**Resolved.** Language: *concluded*, *established*, *the evidence supports*.

> The 1850 census gives Susannah's age as 46 and the 1860 census gives 58, which cannot both be true. The 1860
> page records every age on it as a multiple of five, indicating estimation by the enumerator rather than
> report by the household; the 1850 age is consistent with the 1820 bracket and the 1821 marriage. The 1850
> figure is accepted as the better census evidence, giving a census-derived estimate of about 1804. (Whether
> that estimate survives contact with the family Bible is a separate conflict, below.)

**Resolved with residual doubt.** The honest majority case: you reached an answer and can name what would
still overturn it. Language: *probably*, *the weight of the evidence favours*, *on the present evidence*, then
a sentence naming the record that would settle it.

> The evidence favours Jesse Teale as Susannah's father, and the estate division makes the alternative hard to
> sustain. It does not distinguish a natural daughter from a step-daughter included in the distribution, and
> no record of Jesse Teale's marriage has been found. A Rowan County deed of gift or a Teale Bible record
> would resolve the remaining question.

Residual doubt is not a weakness in the document. An argument that names its own soft spot is more
trustworthy than one that does not, and the reader will find the soft spot anyway.

**Unresolved.** A legitimate research outcome, and writing it up properly is real work, not a failure to hide.
Language: *unresolved*, *the evidence is in conflict*, *two readings remain open*.

> **Unresolved: Susannah's birth year.** The 1850 census implies about 1804. The Cray family Bible records
> 3 February 1799. Neither can be preferred on quality: the census age is secondary information from an
> unidentified informant, and the Bible entry, in one hand in a volume imprinted 1834, was written at least 35
> years after the date it records by an unidentified compiler. A baptismal or delayed-birth record would
> settle it; none is known to survive for Rowan County for this period. Both readings are carried forward, and
> any conclusion depending on Susannah's exact age is stated as depending on it.

Three rules for the unresolved write-up: carry **both** readings forward rather than picking one for
convenience; mark every downstream conclusion that depends on the choice; and name the record type that would
resolve it, so the next researcher has somewhere to start.

**Never counts as resolution:** picking the source that supports the tree you already built; averaging two
dates; citing the majority; silent omission; "sources disagree" with no analysis; or recording both values in
the database with no note that they conflict.

**And hedging is not analysis.** "It seems likely that perhaps she may have been his daughter" sounds
cautious, proves and disproves nothing, and hides that no analysis happened. Say how strong the case is and
why. Hedge once, precisely, with the reason attached.

## 5. The research report

The record of an episode of research: what was asked, what was searched, what was found, what it means, what
to do next. It is the unit of accountability in professional work and the most valuable thing you can leave a
successor. Skeleton to copy: `templates/research-report.md`.

| Section | Contains | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **Header** | client or "self", researcher, date, report number, period covered, scope or hours | makes the report citable as a source in its own right |
| **Objective** | the question verbatim as agreed, plus any limits set on it | what the report is judged against |
| **Summary of findings** | the answers, 3 to 8 sentences, up front | the client reads this; the rest is support |
| **Background** | what was known and believed at the start, with citations, and what was taken on trust | separates new work from inherited assumptions, and catches inherited errors |
| **Sources searched** | every source consulted, with jurisdiction, span, and result, **nil results included** | the reasonably-exhaustive element, made checkable |
| **Findings and analysis** | per find: what it says, source and information evaluation, what it is evidence of, correlation, conflicts | the substance |
| **Conclusions** | what is now concluded, at what confidence, and which proof form each warrants | the fifth GPS element |
| **Unresolved questions** | what remains open and *why* | stops the next researcher repeating your dead ends |
| **Recommended next steps** | prioritised and specific: named record group, jurisdiction, span, repository, what each would establish, effort or cost | turns the report into a plan |

**Client report vs. report to your future self.** Same sections; the audience changes the register only.

| | To a client | To your future self |
|---|---|---|
| Register | gloss the record types and why they matter | shorthand and jargon fine |
| Findings | transcriptions or abstracts included, images attached | pointers to archive filenames suffice |
| Background | restate in full; they do not have your files | restate anyway, briefly; you will not remember |
| Next steps | prioritised with effort and likely cost, so they can choose | prioritised with your reasoning for the priority |
| Nil results | explain what a nil result does and does not mean | list them; otherwise you will re-search them |
| Tone on doubt | plain: "probable, not proved, and here is why" | identical; do not flatter yourself either |

"Write it for your future self" is not a lower bar. Your future self has forgotten everything and cannot ask
you a question, which makes them harder to serve than a client.

**Why the nil-results section is not optional**, any one reason being sufficient: positive findings show
where you looked last, nil results show where you looked at all, so this is how *reasonably exhaustive*
becomes verifiable; a source searched in 2019 and not recorded is a source you search again in 2027; negative
evidence (§1.3) is unavailable without a documented search of stated scope; and for a client, eight hours that
eliminated four record groups is real work that an unreported nil result makes look like nothing.

Record each so it is reusable — source, jurisdiction, exact span, index or page-by-page, variants, condition.
"Searched the 1840 census, nothing" is useless. "Searched 1840 US census, Rowan and Davidson counties, N.C.,
every-name index plus page-by-page review of Rowan; variants Teale, Teal, Teele, Tale; no head of household of
that surname in either county" is reusable for a decade. (*Strategy* for negative searching:
`references/research-workflows.md`; the duty to record them auditably is here.)

**Report vs. compiled genealogy.** Different documents, different jobs; do not let one masquerade as the other.

| | Research report | Compiled genealogy |
|---|---|---|
| Organised by | the process, chronologically by episode | the family, by generation and person |
| Shows | the searching, including failures and blind alleys | the conclusions, with their support |
| Nil results | yes, prominently | no, except where negative evidence is argued |
| Lifespan | a snapshot, superseded by the next report | cumulative, revised in place with versions recorded |
| Conclusions | provisional, confidence stated | settled enough to publish, proof documents behind them |

A compiled genealogy built straight from reports without a proof pass will carry the reports' provisional
language into print as though settled. Do the proof pass.

## 6. Compiled genealogy conventions

Narrative in a numbered descent system, documentation carried in notes. Numbering mechanics, choice of descent
system, and unusual descents: `references/relationship-mapping.md`. The documentation side is here.

**In the narrative:** identity, vital events, residences, occupation, dates; the relationship statements that
structure the descent; conclusions at their actual confidence ("probably" in the text is a promise that the
notes explain why only probably); enough context to make the person legible; and a pointer wherever a
conclusion rests on an argument ("the evidence for this relationship is set out in the proof argument at
[location]"). **In the notes:** all citations; source and information evaluation where it affects weight;
conflicts and their resolution unless central enough for the text; rejected alternative readings, with
reasons; nil results explaining a gap the reader will notice; transcriptions where exact wording matters.

**Citation density.** The scholarly expectation is a note for every assertion of an event, date, place,
relationship, or name variant, and for every quotation, so most sentences in a good sketch carry one. Two
habits keep density high and the page readable. **Bundle within a sentence, never across a paragraph:** one
note covering that sentence's three assertions in order is fine; one note at the end of a paragraph covering
nine is not, because the reader cannot tell which source supports which claim. **Cite the inference too:**
where a statement is your conclusion rather than a record's assertion, say so in the note — "Estimated from her
age in the 1850 census and her 1821 marriage; see [location]" — because an uncited inference sitting among
cited assertions reads as a sourced statement, which is misrepresentation even when unintended.

**Test for a finished sketch:** a reader who disagrees with any single sentence can find, from the notes
alone, the thing they would have to go and look at to check you.

## 7. Data hygiene and the research archive

Your conclusions will outlive your software. Every convention below is chosen to survive export, migration,
and a successor who never met you.

**Names.** Record **as written and separately standardised, and keep both permanently**. The as-written form
is data about the record; the standardised form is your conclusion about the person, and overwriting the first
destroys evidence silently. As written: `Susanah Teal` (1821 bond), `Susannah Teale` (1839 division),
`Susan Cray` (1850 census). Standardised: `Susannah Teale`, later `Susannah (Teale) Cray`. Use the maiden name
as a woman's primary surname with the married form parenthesised in narrative. Where a maiden name is unknown,
leave the field **empty** rather than entering `Unknown`, `?`, or the husband's surname: `Unknown` becomes a
surname the moment you export, and it sorts, indexes, and merges as one. Record every variant you encounter,
attached to the record it came from; variants are the raw material of later searching and later identity
arguments.

**Against ALL CAPS surnames.** The convention marks which name is the surname, and every data format already
has a surname field that does the same job and survives export. Caps destroy internal capitalisation that
carries information (`McDonald`, `FitzGerald`, `de la Vergne`, `van Wely`, `ffrench`) unrecoverably, break
case-sensitive matching, read as shouting, and are spelled letter by letter by some screen readers. If a house
style demands caps, apply them **at output**, never in the data.

**Dates and places** belong to `references/timeline-building.md`: date forms, estimate notation, dual dating
and calendars, date arithmetic, place normalisation, and the jurisdiction-as-at-the-event rule. Two archiving
consequences are mine. **Never store an all-numeric date** — `3/2/1799` is 3 February to a British reader and
2 March to an American one — with **ISO `YYYY-MM-DD` the one exception, and only where machine sorting is the
point: filenames, logs, and record headers such as the conclusion record in §8**, never in narrative and never
in a citation. And record date and place **as the record gives them** beside your standardised interpretation,
keeping both permanently, for the same reason you keep the as-written name.

**Software migration.** Assume at least two more program changes. Once a year and before any migration,
export, import into a different program, and **read the result**: what silently disappears is custom event
types and tags, note formatting, media links, citation detail collapsed into one text blob, research logs, and
to-do items. **Your archive of record is not the software.** It is the images, transcriptions, citations, log,
reports, and proof documents, in open formats, in a folder structure you control; the database is a
convenience for querying and charting. Keep one plain-text or PDF rendering of current conclusions with their
citations, regenerated when they change. That is the copy that still opens in thirty years.

**The folder structure**, since the archive of record is a folder tree and "the filing scheme in plain
language" has to refer to something. One workable layout, to adapt rather than copy:

```
ARCHIVE-ROOT/
  START-HERE.md            <- read first: scope, filing scheme, current conclusions, open questions
  images/                  <- one folder per jurisdiction+record group; sidecar .txt beside each image
    rowan-co-nc_estates/   <- plus index.csv per folder: filename, citation, date captured
  transcriptions/          <- full transcripts and abstracts, plain text or Markdown, one per record
  proofs/                  <- proof statements, summaries, arguments; superseded versions kept, marked
    superseded/
  reports/                 <- research reports, numbered and dated; delivered versions never edited
  logs/                    <- research log (incl. nil results), backup-restore log, consent register
  correspondence/          <- letters and email that carry evidence or permissions, dated, as PDF
  permissions/             <- what you asked, of whom, their reply, and the terms attached
  conclusions/             <- the conclusion records of section 8, one per ID, plus a current index
  dna/                     <- kit inventory, match correspondence, consent records; access-restricted
  database-exports/        <- dated GEDCOM or equivalent, plus a dated PDF of current conclusions
```

Whatever layout you choose, it must satisfy three tests: a stranger can tell from a folder name what is in it;
nothing lives only inside the genealogy program; and every image can be reunited with its citation without
opening the program (`index.csv` and sidecars, below).

**Image capture: capture the context, not just the entry.** The commonest archive failure is a folder of
beautiful crops nobody can cite. Per record, in order: the **finding aid or catalog entry** that got you there
(this is what makes the citation reconstructable); the **title page or spine** showing volume and date span;
the **full page or opening**, uncropped, with page numbers and stamped foliation visible, plus the facing page
where the record crosses the gutter; the **entry itself**, cropped, as a *second* file and never the only one;
**one page either side**, because sequence, hand changes, and clerical patterns are evidence and you cannot go
back; and the **index page** pointing to the entry. Highest resolution available, lossless where permitted,
square to the sensor, with lighting or damage that defeated legibility noted.

**File naming.** A filename is a handle, not a citation, but a good handle sorts usefully and never collides:

```
YYYY-MM-DD_jurisdiction_recordtype_volume-page_surname-given_descriptor.ext

2026-03-04_rowan-co-nc_estate-file_1838_teale-jesse_division-p1.jpg
2026-03-04_rowan-co-nc_estate-file_1838_teale-jesse_folder-label.jpg
2026-03-04_rowan-co-nc_guardian-bonds_v3_nil-search_1838-1841.jpg
1921-03-18_allegheny-co-pa_death-cert_vardy-susannah_full.jpg
1921-03-18_allegheny-co-pa_death-cert_vardy-susannah_detail-informant.jpg
```

ISO dates so filenames sort chronologically; lowercase throughout; hyphens inside a component, underscores
between; no spaces, `&`, `#`, `/`, or accented characters; surname before given name; a descriptor saying
which part of the record this is. Pick one rule for whether the prefix is the record's date or the capture
date and hold it. Note that the nil search gets a file too.

**Metadata.** The filename cannot hold the citation, so attach it twice: a sidecar text file of the same
basename carrying the full reference note, repository and address, access date, call or file number, condition
of the original, and what the image does not show; **and** embedded IPTC/XMP description and source fields
written at import, which travel with the file but are invisible in a listing and stripped by some converters.
Plus one plain-text or CSV index per folder listing every image with its citation. The index is what a
successor reads first.

**Backup: three copies, two media, one offsite, with a verified restore.** Two genealogy-specific points
carry the rule. A synchronised cloud folder is **not** a backup: it propagates your deletions and your
ransomware faithfully. And the verified restore is the only part that proves the rest — quarterly, restore a
random file and open it; annually, restore a whole folder; log the date and result. **An untested backup is a
hypothesis about a backup.** Keep backups in open formats and encrypt anything holding living people's data,
recording where the key is in a way your executor can act on.

**What a successor needs.** Keep a current `START-HERE` document at the archive root: what the archive covers
(families, surnames, places, periods); the filing scheme in plain language; the current conclusions and where
their proof documents live; the superseded-conclusions log (§8), so they know what was tried and rejected; the
open questions and prioritised next steps from the latest report; the research log, whole and unpruned; where
the physical originals are, in whose custody, and what you promised about them; which subscription sites,
societies, and repositories hold trees, DNA kits, or deposits, and how to reach them (name where credentials
are held, never write them into the archive); and the privacy and consent register (§9).

**The disposition plan**, in `START-HERE`, in two or three sentences, because an archive with no named
destination is an archive that goes into a skip. Name the repository, genealogical or historical society,
library, or family member the archive is to go to, with a contact and the date you last confirmed they will
accept it; say whether a deed of gift, deposit agreement, or bequest already exists, where the signed copy is
held, and what conditions it carries (access restrictions, the fate of living-people data, whether the
depositor may be named); and say what happens to the digital archive specifically, since repositories that
take paper often will not take files. If nothing is signed, write "no agreement yet" rather than leaving it
blank, so a successor knows it is an open task and not a settled one. Revisit it whenever the archive's
contents or the recipient's terms change.

## 8. Conclusions change

A conclusion is a statement about the current state of the evidence. New evidence changes it. That is not a
failure of the original work, and concealing the change is not a courtesy to yourself.

**Never overwrite silently.** Overwriting destroys the reasoning, invites the rejected conclusion to be
re-adopted later by you or someone else, and makes it impossible to tell whether a difference between two
versions of your work is a correction or an error.

```
CONCLUSION RECORD
ID:             C-014          Status: Current       First stated: 2024-06-11
Question:       Who was the father of Susannah Teale, m. Bartholomew Cray 1821?
Conclusion:     Jesse Teale of Rowan County, North Carolina.
Confidence:     Probable, residual doubt as to natural vs. step relationship.
Proof document: proofs/C-014_teale-susannah-parentage_v2.md     Revised: 2026-03-04
Supersedes:     C-014 v1 (2024-06-11), which concluded William Teale.
Reason:         Rowan County estate file for Jesse Teale (1838) located 2026-02-28. The
                report of division awards a share to Bartholomew Cray in right of his wife,
                inconsistent with William Teale's paternity. v1 relied on Pell, Annals of
                Rowan County (1897), now shown undocumented and contradicted by William
                Teale's 1841 will.
Depends on:     C-009 (identity: 1821 bride = woman in B. Cray's household 1830-1860).
Depended on by: C-021 (Teale line to the 1750s), C-030 (DNA: shared segments with the Teale
                descendant cluster), Report 2025-03, published sketch for generation 4.
Propagated:     2026-03-06, [researcher] — all five dependents re-tested; C-021 withdrawn.
```

**Keep v1.** The superseded proof document stays in the archive under its own version, marked
`SUPERSEDED 2026-03-04, see C-014 v2`. It is not deleted and not edited into agreement with v2. Someone will
find the 1897 county history again and needs to know it was already considered.

**Propagate the revision.** This is the hard step, and why `Depended on by` exists. (1) **List the dependents
before changing anything**: other conclusions, charts, numbered sketches, delivered reports, published trees,
lineage society applications, DNA hypotheses, correspondence. (2) **Re-test each.** A dependent does not
automatically fall, but it must be re-argued, not assumed to survive; above, C-021's whole ascent moves to a
different man. (3) **Correct what is out in the world, visibly**: an issued report gets an erratum or a
superseding report naming what changed and why, sent to everyone who received the original; a published page
gets a dated correction note, not a silent edit; an application in progress gets an amendment, one already
accepted gets a notification. (4) **Notify the people you told** — cousins, collaborators, the person whose
DNA test you interpreted. You persuaded them; you owe them the retraction. (5) **Date and initial the
propagation** in the record, so a successor sees it was done rather than intended.

A revision handled this way strengthens your credibility. One discovered by someone else because two of your
documents disagree destroys it.

## 9. Ethics and legal constraints, as protocol

Handling rules, not warnings.

**Living people.** Treat a person as living unless you hold evidence of death or they were born before a
documented cutoff you set and apply consistently; write the cutoff into `START-HERE` so the practice is
reviewable. Keep **full data in the private archive**: privacy protection concerns publication, not research.
In anything shared, the default is name withheld — `Living`, initials, or given name plus initial — with birth
dates, addresses, and identifiers useful for impersonation suppressed, for the living and the recently
deceased. **Consent unlocks publication and nothing else does**, and it is specific: *this* information, in
*this* publication, in *this* form, logged in the consent register with date and wording. Consent to appear in
a tree emailed to cousins is not consent to appear on a public website. For minors a parent or guardian
consents and a higher suppression default applies regardless. Jurisdiction: US practice is a patchwork of
state law and site policy; the UK and EU analogue is data protection law (UK GDPR and the GDPR), which treats
identifiable living people's data as regulated personal data carrying rights of access and erasure, and which
reaches your spreadsheet as surely as a company's database. If someone asks to be removed, remove them from
the published product promptly, record the request, and do not argue.

**Sensitive findings, general protocol.** (1) **Verify before disclosing**; a misattributed parentage asserted
on weak evidence and retracted does damage the retraction will not undo. (2) **Tell the most affected living
person first, privately, before it appears in any circulated document** — for client work, tell them before
the written report goes out, rather than letting them read it there. (3) **Write it neutrally, cite it
precisely, and stop**: record what the record says, and do not speculate about motive, morality, or feelings.
(4) **Restrict circulation by default**: the private archive and the report to the person entitled to it, not
the published tree. (5) **When a suppressed conclusion is load-bearing, say so in place of it.** Withheld
evidence is routinely the evidence a lineage application, a portfolio submission, or a published descent
actually stands on, and the two dishonest exits are publishing it anyway and restating the descent as though
the link needed no support. Take neither. Write into the document itself that the relationship rests on
evidence withheld at the request of a living person, name who holds that evidence and in what form, and state
that it is available to a reviewer, judge, or society examiner under confidentiality on request. Then ask the
person whose privacy is at stake whether that much disclosure is acceptable, and record the answer and its
date in the consent register. If they decline even that, the application or the publication proceeds without
the link and says the link is unproved **on the evidence this document may show** — which is true — or it does
not proceed. It never proceeds on evidence the reader is not told exists.

| Category | Specific handling |
|---|---|
| **Misattributed parentage** (documentary or DNA) | Tell the test-taker or closest affected living person first and alone. Do not tell other relatives, including the ones who commissioned you: the person who paid is not entitled to a third party's parentage. Write it as an evidentiary conclusion, never an accusation. Expect to be asked to keep it unpublished, and agree. |
| **Crime, punishment, transportation** | Record it; it is history and usually well documented. Cite the court or prison record rather than a newspaper where you can. Avoid moral framing and modern legal vocabulary for historical offences. Check closure periods. |
| **Institutionalisation, asylum, workhouse, illness** | Many jurisdictions close these for a fixed period; the length varies, so check the specific holding rather than assuming a number. Respect the closure even when a copy has leaked online. Do not apply modern diagnoses to historical descriptions. Suppress details for anyone within living memory unless the family asks otherwise. |
| **Illegitimacy, adoption, care, name changes** | Historical is generally publishable with care; within living memory, suppress unless consented. Adoption records are sealed in many jurisdictions, and the seal binds you even if a relative shares the contents. |

**Enslavement records: the duty of care**, different in kind rather than degree. **Name people**: where a
record names an enslaved person, record that name as a *person*, in your index and database, with the care you
give anyone else, rather than leaving them an unnamed count in a property inventory. **Record the enslaver's
name in the same entry**, so the link is searchable both ways; descendant researchers search by enslaver
surname because that is often the only handle the records leave them, so make your work findable that way.
**Reproduce the record's information, not its framing**: cite the inventory, bill of sale, will, or slave
schedule precisely, and in your own prose write about people rather than property, avoiding the passive
constructions that erase who acted — "Jesse Teale's 1838 estate inventory lists Dinah, aged about 40, and her
children Peter and Sarah, appraised together at $800" rather than "the estate included three slaves."
**Apply the same evidence analysis**: the informant for nearly every assertion in these records is the
enslaver, an appraiser, or a clerk acting for them, with a financial interest in the valuation and none in
accuracy about names, ages, kinship, or origin; ages in inventories are appraisal estimates, and family
groupings are recorded when they affect value and omitted when they do not. Classify accordingly and say so.
**Share findings outward**: these records are of far more use to the descendants of the enslaved than to the
enslaver's, so contribute names and citations to the collaborative databases built for that purpose and answer
descendant researchers who contact you. Living descendants get the living-people rules in full. And **do not
narrate around it**: a family history detailing a man's land and livestock while passing over the people he
held is a documentation failure as well as a moral one.

**DNA.** Correlation mechanics: `references/relationship-mapping.md`. The consent protocol is here, and it is
two consents, not one. *Consent to test*: before the sample is given, the taker understands in their own words
that results may reveal unexpected close relationships — misattributed parentage, unknown siblings, donor
conception — in their family and in others'; that matching against other testers is the point; who holds and
controls the account and the raw data; what the company's terms permit, including transfer and any
law-enforcement matching, and whether those are opt-in; and that the data outlives the test and may be
inherited or sold with the company. Record it. A minor's parent or guardian consents, with the higher
suppression default; for a deceased or incapacitated person's sample the holder of legal authority decides,
and what that person would have wanted is still yours to weigh. *Consent to publish is separate and
specific*: being in your match list is not consent to be named in your proof argument. Settle in advance
whether a match is named in full, by initials, by code (`Match A`), or not at all; whether shared cM totals
and segment data appear, since shared cM is data about a relationship and so partly theirs; whether their
tree, stated ancestry, or correspondence may be quoted; and whether publication is private,
society-restricted, or public, consent to one being no consent to another. The defensible default in a
published argument is pseudonymised labels for living matches, full cM values, the relationship path stated,
and the underlying identifications held privately and available to a reviewer or portfolio judge under
confidentiality. Say in the document that you have done this.

**Copyright, fair use, and repository restrictions** are three separate constraints; clearing one does not
clear the others. Copyright protects **expression, not facts**: a 1921 certificate's information is free, a
modern author's prose about it is not, and some jurisdictions treat a skilled reproduction of a public-domain
document more generously than the United States does. **Database and compilation rights** may protect
selection and arrangement where the items themselves are free; the EU and UK recognise a database right with
no US equivalent. **Contract and repository restriction bind independently of copyright** — a subscription
site's terms, a reader agreement, a "no publication without written permission" clause on a photography form
— and this is the constraint that actually catches genealogists, because it reaches public-domain records.
**US fair use** weighs purpose, nature, amount, and market effect; the UK, Irish, Canadian, and Australian
analogue is **fair dealing**, narrower and limited to enumerated purposes. Practically: quote sparingly,
transcribe freely, seek permission before publishing an image you did not create, use the repository's
required acknowledgement exactly as given, never republish a subscription site's images publicly, and keep a
permissions file recording what you asked, of whom, what they said, and when.


**Attribution.** **Cite the researcher** by name and medium for anything you learned from them and have not
re-derived from the original: email, conference paper, message board post, database entry, private
compilation, conversation, with the date and how it reached you. **"Found in an online tree" is not a citation
and not a source**; it is a lead. Go to the record the tree points at and cite that. If the tree asserts
something with no underlying record, cite it as an authored narrative, name its compiler if named, and flag
the assertion as unverified in your own text. **Copying a conclusion is copying an argument**: if another
researcher's reasoning solved your problem, say so in the text, not only in a note, because repackaging
someone else's proof argument as your own analysis is plagiarism even when every underlying record is public.
**Merging trees** imports other people's unreviewed conclusions into yours: treat every imported assertion as
unverified until re-tested against a source you examined, and flag imported material so you can tell it apart
later. And if you circulated someone else's mistake, your correction goes to everyone you circulated it to
(§8).
